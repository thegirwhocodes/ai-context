### How the FDIC actually got its money back after SVB

**1. The hole that needed filling.** When SVB (March 10, 2023) and Signature Bank (March 12, 2023) failed, the Treasury, Fed, and FDIC invoked the **systemic risk exception** under the FDIC Improvement Act. This let them protect *all* depositors — including uninsured deposits above the $250K cap — instead of doing the usual "least-cost resolution." The Deposit Insurance Fund (DIF) absorbed the loss. The FDIC estimated this cost the DIF roughly **$16.3 billion** (later revised to ~$16.7B as of Sep 2025), almost all of it from covering uninsured depositors.

**2. The legal trigger for repayment.** By statute, when the FDIC uses the systemic risk exception, it *must* recover the resulting losses from the banking industry — not from taxpayers. So in November 2023 the FDIC Board issued a **final rule on a "special assessment."**

**3. Who pays — the assessment base.**
- The base is each bank's **estimated uninsured deposits** as reported on its December 31, 2022 call report.
- The first **$5 billion** of uninsured deposits is **excluded** per banking organization. So small community banks with little uninsured-deposit exposure pay zero.
- This was a deliberate design choice: the banks that *benefited* from the systemic-risk bailout (because their uninsured depositors got protected) were the ones with lots of uninsured deposits. So the assessment falls on them, not on small banks.

**4. The rate — "13.4 bps."**
- "bps" = **basis points**. 1 bp = 0.01%. So **13.4 bps = 0.134%** per year.
- The FDIC charges this as an *annual* rate, but collects it **quarterly** at 3.36 bps per quarter (one-quarter of 13.4).
- It started Q1 2024 and runs for **eight quarterly assessment periods (≈ 2 years)** to spread the burden.

**5. The arithmetic.**
- Total uninsured deposits at large U.S. banks (after the $5B-per-bank exclusion) ≈ ~$1.2 trillion.
- 0.134% × $1.2T ≈ **$16B per year of base** → over 2 years ≈ $16–17B, matching the loss.

**6. Who actually pays the most.** The big winners from the SVB rescue — JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, Citigroup, and other banks with large corporate / institutional uninsured-deposit bases — pay almost all of it. Roughly 95% of the special assessment falls on the ~110 banks with assets above $50 billion.

**7. Why this matters for your DD framing.** In the model, when the DI fund pays out `c_1*` to depositors during a run, the resources have to come from *somewhere*. The 2023 episode shows the actual repayment chain:

```
Run happens
   ↓
DIF pays uninsured depositors (immediate liquidity from DIF + $40B borrowed from Treasury)
   ↓
DIF re-bills the surviving banks ex-post (special assessment, 13.4 bps × 2 years)
   ↓
Surviving banks pay → DIF rebuilt → Treasury loan repaid
```

So in real-life DD terms, **the "deposit insurance fund g" is partly a revolving credit line on the surviving banks themselves.** The Treasury bridges the timing gap, but the ultimate payer is the banking industry, not the taxpayer. This is exactly why FDIC can claim "no taxpayer money was used" — the Treasury's $40B advance was repaid by March 15, 2023, days after it was drawn.

**Why this is relevant to your paper.** Your proposal is essentially asking: instead of the surviving-banks-repay-ex-post mechanism (which assumes a healthy banking system after the crisis — not always true), can foreign investors *pre-fund* the DIF so the burden never falls on US banks or the Treasury at all? The 13.4 bps story is the existing answer; your paper proposes an alternative one.

### Why US banks would *want* foreign money in the DIF, and how the foreigners get paid back

These are two separate questions. Let me take them in order.

---

## Part 1: Why US banks (and the FDIC) would prefer foreign capital over the status quo

The current system has US banks paying for the DIF in two ways:
- **Ex-ante regular assessments** (~3–32 bps annually on assessment base)
- **Ex-post special assessments** when the DIF takes a hit (the 13.4 bps × 2 years SVB episode)

These are real costs that hit bank earnings. Foreign-funded DI changes who bears those costs. The incentives, in order of bite:

**(1) Lower assessment rates.** If foreign capital `K` raises the fund toward `g^T` without raising domestic premia, the FDIC can cut the regular assessment rate. **JPMorgan paid ~$3 billion in FDIC assessments in 2023.** A material cut is real money for shareholders. Banks lobby aggressively over much smaller assessment changes than this — the incentive is large and concentrated.

**(2) No ex-post surprise assessments.** The 2023 special assessment cost the largest banks ~$15 billion combined, hitting Q4 2023 earnings. JPMorgan alone took a $2.9B charge; BofA $1.9B; Wells $1.9B. These are *not* in budgets — they show up as one-time hits to EPS. Banks hate this because it makes earnings volatile and management compensation harder to defend. If the foreign-funded fund is large enough, special assessments become unnecessary.

**(3) Bigger insured-deposit ceiling without more cost.** Post-SVB there's strong political pressure to raise the $250K cap (or do "targeted" coverage of business transaction accounts). The FDIC's 2023 Options report says raising the cap to even $1M would require ~$50–100B more in the fund. Foreign capital lets banks support a higher cap *without* paying for it — and a higher cap means more deposits stay in banks instead of fleeing to MMFs (a real concern after 2023).

**(4) Cheaper deposit funding.** Better-capitalized DI = lower run risk = depositors accept lower interest rates. There's empirical work (Diamond-Dybvig 1983 itself, plus Pennacchi 2006 in *J. Monetary Economics*) showing depositors price the perceived safety of the DI scheme. A more credible scheme means banks pay less for deposits. **This is the biggest and most underappreciated incentive.**

**(5) Avoiding the diabolic loop.** If a future crisis hits and Treasury yields spike, banks holding Treasuries take mark-to-market losses (this is exactly what happened to SVB). A foreign-funded DIF reduces the Treasury's contingent liabilities, so Treasury yields are lower in stress, so bank balance sheets stay healthier. Banks *care* about this — JPMorgan's CFO has been explicit about Treasury-yield risk in earnings calls.

**(6) Reduced regulatory friction.** With a more credible DIF, the case for *additional* capital and liquidity requirements (Basel III Endgame, the post-SVB rules being negotiated now in 2026) gets weaker. Banks fight every basis point of higher capital requirements; foreign-funded DI is a way to get the same financial-stability benefit at lower regulatory cost to them.

The cleanest framing for your paper: **foreign capital is a substitute for domestic bank assessments, and US banks prefer the substitution because their cost of funding falls.**

---

## Part 2: How foreigners get paid back — three mechanisms, ranked by realism

Recall from Section 4 of the memo: their participation constraint is `expected return ≥ R_F` (their outside option, e.g., Treasury yields).

### Mechanism A — Insurance premia paid by US banks (the cleanest)

US banks continue to pay assessments to the FDIC, but at a **lower rate** than today. Some fraction of that premium income is **passed through to foreign capital providers** as a coupon.

- Foreign investor deposits `K` at `t=0`.
- US banks pay annual premium `α` to the DIF (currently ~5 bps average).
- DIF pays foreign investors `ρ_DI · K` per period out of those premia.
- At `t=2` (or contract maturity), if no run occurred, foreigners get principal `K` back.
- If a run occurred, `K` is depleted to pay depositors (foreigners take first loss).

This is essentially **the foreigners are buying a long-dated, high-yield CD from the FDIC, junior to insured depositors.** The FDIC already issues debt under Section 15 of the FDI Act, so the legal infrastructure exists.

The pricing math, with run probability `p`:
$$\rho_{DI} = R_F + p \cdot L / (1-p)$$

where `L` is the loss given run. If your model successfully kills the run equilibrium, `p ≈ 0` in the good NE, so `ρ_DI ≈ R_F` — foreigners need almost zero premium over Treasuries.

**This is the mechanism I recommend you formalize in the paper.** It's clean, it has institutional precedent (FDIC bond issuance authority), and the participation constraint is easy to derive.

### Mechanism B — Reciprocal coverage of foreign banks (the EDIS model)

Foreign DGSs (deposit guarantee schemes from other countries) contribute to a joint pool. **In return, their banks' depositors get coverage from the pool** when needed.

- Japan's DGS contributes `K_J`, gets a draw right of up to `K_J + α·K_pool` if Japanese banks fail.
- The "return" is **insurance against Japan's own banking crisis** — purely an in-kind benefit, no cash interest.
- Cross-country diversification: if Japanese, US, and European banking shocks are imperfectly correlated, the pool can cover any one country's crisis with less total capital than each country would need alone (law of large numbers, the same logic that makes the DD bank better than autarky).

**This is how EDIS was designed to work** (reinsurance phase → coinsurance → full insurance, see Section 3.6 of the memo). The "return" is the access right, not a cash flow.

The downside: free-riding. Strong-banking countries don't want to subsidize weak-banking ones. **EDIS has been stuck for 9 years on exactly this problem.** Your paper should acknowledge this honestly.

### Mechanism C — Senior tranche of a cross-border safe asset (ESBies analog)

The DI pool issues a **senior tranche** that foreign investors buy. The senior tranche is protected by:
- Diversification across multiple countries' DI obligations
- Subordination to a junior tranche absorbing first losses

Foreign investors get:
- A genuinely safe dollar-denominated asset, paying close to but slightly above Treasuries
- Senior status — they only take losses in the worst tail outcomes

This is Brunnermeier et al.'s (2016) ESBies architecture, applied to deposit insurance instead of sovereign debt.

**Why this beats just buying Treasuries:** the senior tranche is *not* a sovereign claim. It's a claim on the pool of bank assessments + foreign contributions. So foreigners get exposure to the US banking system's premium income without taking sovereign risk. This is genuinely a different asset from a Treasury bond.

The downside: requires creating a new international issuer (analogous to the European Stability Mechanism). Politically heavy lift.

---

## Part 3: The "fee for service" framing that holds it all together

Here's the way to think about it that makes both questions click:

**The DI fund is an insurance product. US banks are the policyholders. Foreigners are reinsurers.**

In commercial insurance markets, primary insurers (US banks) pay premia to reinsurers (foreign investors) in exchange for catastrophic-loss coverage. Reinsurers earn a profit in normal times and absorb losses in catastrophic times. This is exactly Munich Re's, Swiss Re's, Lloyd's of London's business model — and it works at scale ($600B global reinsurance market).

Your proposal is: **make the FDIC a primary insurer that buys reinsurance from foreign sovereign wealth funds, foreign DGSs, or international institutions** (BIS, IMF). The premium flow is from US banks → FDIC → foreign reinsurers. The risk flow is the reverse.

This framing has three big advantages for the paper:
1. **Existing market analog**: reinsurance is a well-understood, well-priced market. You can reference how Munich Re prices catastrophe risk.
2. **Clear participation constraint**: foreign reinsurers participate at premia consistent with their cost of capital.
3. **Resolves the "what do they get" question instantly**: they get the same thing reinsurers always get — premium income in normal times, willingness to bear tail risk in exchange.

The 1991 FDICIA already directed the FDIC to study **private reinsurance** (per the FDIC Reform Report). They never implemented it because the domestic reinsurance market wasn't deep enough. **Foreign reinsurers — sovereign wealth funds with $13 trillion AUM, foreign central banks with $12 trillion in reserves — are deep enough.**

---

## Part 4: How this lands in your DD model

Concretely, in the model setup from Section 5 of the memo:

- Foreign investors `F` deposit `K` into the DIF at `t=0`, in exchange for a contingent contract.
- US banks pay assessments `α·D` per period (where `D` = total domestic deposits) into the DIF.
- The DIF pays foreigners coupon `ρ_DI · K` per period out of those assessments.
- At `t=2`:
  - **No-run state**: foreigners receive `K · (1 + ρ_DI)`. Banks have paid total `α·D · 2` over the two periods. DIF nets the remainder for next-period equity.
  - **Run state**: foreigners receive `max(K − DIF_loss, 0)`. Banks face a special assessment.
- Foreigners' participation: `(1−p)·(1 + ρ_DI) + p · max(0, 1 − loss/K) ≥ R_F`
- Banks' participation: `α·D ≤ savings from foreign capital + cost of run reduction`

**The model's key result** (this is your contribution): there's a range of parameters where:
1. Foreigners participate (their constraint binds at `R_F`)
2. US banks prefer this to the status quo (lower `α` than current FDIC assessments)
3. The combined fund `g_dom + K > g^T` (run equilibrium is killed)
4. The Treasury's contingent debt does not increase (diabolic loop weakened)

That's a Pareto improvement, parameterized cleanly, and it's exactly the kind of result that justifies a 7-page paper.

---

## Bottom line

- **US banks' incentive**: lower assessments + cheaper deposit funding + earnings smoothing + reduced regulatory pressure. The cost savings are concrete, billions per year for the largest banks.
- **Foreigners' return**: they're functionally **reinsurers** of US deposit insurance. They earn premium income (paid by US banks via assessments) in normal times; they bear first-loss in a crisis. Yield premium over Treasuries compensates for the tail risk.
- **The reinsurance framing** is the right one to use in your paper because it has real-world analogs (Munich Re, Lloyd's), a clear participation constraint, and an immediate institutional path (the 1991 FDICIA already authorized the FDIC to study private reinsurance — your contribution is "foreign reinsurance").

The government can credibly do this because (a) it has taxation power, and (b) its citizens get the benefit of a stable banking system.
A foreign investor contributing to a US DI fund has neither of these. They're putting capital at risk to absorb losses on deposits they don't own, in a banking system whose stability primarily benefits US depositors. So the question "what do they gain?" isn't a small detail — it's the question that determines whether the mechanism exists at all.
The realistic answers, and why each is shaky
A return/yield on contributed capital. This is the natural answer, but it pushes you straight into a contradiction. If foreign contributors earn a return, the DI fund must generate income — either by charging US banks higher premiums (which banks pass to depositors, undermining the stability gain) or by investing the fund in risky assets (which means the insurance fund itself becomes run-prone, just like Circle's reserves at SVB on slide 11). You'd essentially be recreating the stablecoin fragility problem inside the insurance mechanism.
Access to USD-denominated safe assets. Foreign investors — especially from emerging markets with capital controls or unstable currencies — already pay a premium for dollar safety (this is the "exorbitant privilege" literature). You could frame the DI contribution as a vehicle for them to hold a claim on a dollar-backed instrument. The gain is the dollar exposure itself, not a yield. This is more defensible but raises the question: why not just buy Treasuries? You'd need to argue the DI claim offers something Treasuries don't — perhaps a higher yield in exchange for the tail risk of being called on during a crisis.
Insurance reciprocity / systemic stability. If the contributing countries' banks are exposed to the US system (correspondent banking, dollar funding markets, USD-denominated cross-border claims), then preventing US bank runs has spillover benefits for them. This is the strongest economic argument, but it's a public-goods problem — each country wants others to contribute. You'd need a coordination mechanism, which is essentially what the IMF or BIS already partially provides through swap lines.
Where DD pushes back hardest
The deeper issue is sequential service. In DD, runs happen because depositors who withdraw early get paid in full while late ones get nothing. Deposit insurance solves this by guaranteeing late payment. But foreign DI contributors are themselves a junior claimant whose payout depends on the fund not being exhausted. So now you have a layered run problem: when US depositors run, the DI fund pays out, foreign contributors absorb losses, and if foreign contributors anticipate this, they may run from the DI fund itself (withdrawing their contributions before a crisis). You've potentially added a new coordination failure rather than eliminated one — which actually echoes Araujo, Izumi, and Mattesini (2026) on slide 12: adding another source of coordination failure can increase fragility.

The DD model has six places you can modify
Let me walk through the standard DD setup and flag each insertion point:
1. The agent population. DD has agents of two types — "type 1" (impatient, want to consume in period 1) and "type 2" (patient, willing to wait until period 2). You can add a third type: foreign investors who are patient but have a different motivation for depositing. 2. The endowment structure. Each agent starts with 1 unit of endowment. - If it captures truly new demand from currently-unbanked EM savers: larger effect

This is essentially the **"exorbitant privilege" mechanism** (Eichengreen, Gourinchas) accelerated. The US already benefits from being the world's reserve issuer; your structure deepens that benefit by extending it to retail. Politically that's good for the US — it's why Treasury Secretary Yellen and the Trump-era GENIUS Act both wanted to *expand* dollar use globally, not contract it.

**Counter-effect:** if EM countries see their citizens fleeing to DepositCoin en masse, they may impose new capital controls (Argentina's cepo was exactly this). That would slow adoption but doesn't damage the US.

### Channel B: Inflation impact (small, mostly neutral)

This is the question your group will get hammered on. The answer is more nuanced than people assume.

When a Nigerian user deposits naira and receives DepositCoin:
- The naira goes to a forex desk that converts it to dollars
- Those dollars are deposited at FDIC-administered reserves
- The reserves are invested in **short-term US Treasuries**

**Key insight:** Treasuries already exist. Buying them doesn't create new dollars — it shifts the holders. So **no monetary base expansion, no direct inflation pressure.**

What *can* affect inflation:
- **Demand-side:** A stronger dollar imports cheaper goods → mildly disinflationary in the US
- **Treasury market:** More demand for short-term Treasuries → lower yields → cheaper US government borrowing (positive fiscal externality)
- **Bank funding:** US banks gain a cheaper backstop, can offer slightly better deposit rates → marginally *more* lending → mild inflationary push

Net effect on inflation: **probably ±0.1% per year, dominated by other factors.** This is small enough to not be a real critique.

### Channel C: Treasury yields (clearly positive for the US)

$150B+ of additional sticky Treasury demand from retail-pooled foreign capital → **slightly lower Treasury yields across the curve.** This is good for the US fiscal position, especially given that current debt service is ~$1T/year.

CRS estimates suggest every 25bp drop in Treasury yields saves ~$70B in interest costs over a decade. Your structure could plausibly contribute 5–15bp of yield compression. **Real money.**

### Channel D: Banking system stability (the actual point)

This is the headline benefit. With `g_dom + K > g^T`:
- Bank runs become rationally impossible (the run NE is eliminated)
- Banks face lower expected funding stress
- Deposit assessment rates can fall (as we discussed — multi-billion in annual savings for big banks)
- Treasury never has to invoke a systemic risk exception again

### Quick answer: yes, in target — but you should design with eligibility tiers

**The honest answer in one paragraph:** Yes, DepositCoin is conceptually worldwide — anyone with a phone and local currency could in principle deposit. But for the paper you should specify a tiered eligibility structure, because "literally every country on Earth" creates real legal and political problems that weaken the model.

## Three eligibility tiers (this is how to handle it in the paper)

**Tier 1 — Open access (the core market).** Nigeria, Argentina, Brazil, Indonesia, Turkey, Philippines, India, Mexico, South Africa, Kenya, Vietnam, etc. Non-sanctioned, non-FATF-blacklisted, with documented retail USD demand. **This is your target user base** — the 47% of crypto users in those countries who already report saving in USD as their primary motivation.

**Tier 2 — Restricted access.** Countries on FATF gray lists, countries with active capital-flight stress that might object, or countries that may impose retaliatory restrictions. Access could require local central bank coordination (e.g., the CBN partners with the FDIC) rather than direct retail signup.

**Tier 3 — Blocked.** US-sanctioned countries (Russia, Iran, North Korea, Cuba, Syria, etc.). DepositCoin would be subject to OFAC sanctions like every other USD-denominated instrument. Not optional — this is US law.

## Why "worldwide" needs caveats

**Legal:** AML/KYC compliance is unavoidable for a US-administered claim. The FDIC can't accept anonymous deposits from anywhere in the world; users would have to identify themselves to a partnered local fintech. This isn't a bug — it's how every legal USD instrument works (including SWIFT, correspondent banking, Treasuries).

**Political:** If you say "worldwide" and someone asks "so Russia could fund the FDIC?", you've lost the room. **Saying "open to retail users in non-sanctioned countries with appropriate AML/KYC compliance" is bulletproof.** Same answer the FDIC gives for its existing operations.

**Practical:** Some countries (China most notably) will likely ban their citizens from holding DepositCoin domestically, because it directly competes with their own currency. China already bans USDC and USDT. Your paper should acknowledge that adoption in any specific country depends on that country's permission, not just US policy.

## Why this strengthens the model rather than weakens it

Tiered eligibility is actually **good for the diversification argument.** Your model relies on imperfectly correlated shocks across source countries — the law-of-large-numbers logic that makes the bank work in DD. **A pool drawing from 30–50 non-sanctioned, geographically and economically diverse countries** has much better diversification than a pool drawing from "everywhere including sanctioned states." So the tiered structure isn't a limitation — it's a feature that makes the no-second-stage-run result tighter.

## How to phrase it in the paper / presentation

> *"DepositCoin is structured as an open-access claim available to retail users in non-sanctioned jurisdictions worldwide, subject to AML/KYC verification through partnered local fintechs. We model the foreign user base as a continuum of small retail participants drawn from a diversified set of source countries (Nigeria, Argentina, Brazil, Indonesia, Turkey, Philippines, India, Mexico, etc.) whose depreciation factors ε are imperfectly correlated, ensuring that pool-level shocks are well-diversified."*

That sentence handles the worldwide question, the legal question, and the diversification argument all at once. It's the version I'd put in the methodology section.

**Worldwide in design, tiered in implementation.** The pool needs scale to work (your `K*` threshold), so you want the broadest possible eligibility — but the broadest *legally and politically defensible* eligibility, not literally everyone. ### Equation 1 — The run-elimination threshold

Start from Lecture 11. The condition for patient depositors to prefer waiting over running is:

```
c₂(e)  >  c₁*    for all e ∈ [0, 1−π]
```

The function `c₂(e)` is the per-capita payment to remaining patient depositors when `e` extra patient depositors panic. From Lecture 11:

```
c₂(e)  =  [(1−π)·c₂*  −  e·(R/r)·c₁*]  /  (1 − π − e)
```

We want this to exceed `c₁*` for the worst case `e = 1−π` (everyone runs). With a deposit insurance fund of size `g`, the bank gets an extra `g` resources to top up payments. Rearranging:

```
g  >  (1 − π)·c₁*  −  r·x*
   =  (1 − π)·(c₁* − (r/R)·c₂*)     [using (1−π)c₂* = R·x*]
   ≡  g^T
```

**With log utility** in DD, the efficient allocation is `(c₁*, c₂*) = (1, R)`. Substituting:

```
E[v(USD payout)]  ≥  E[v(local·ε)]

```


Rearranging:

```
p·τ  ≤  1 − E[ε]
```

### What `R·τ·x* < D₀ + g` means

This is the **default condition** from Izumi (2020) — the inequality that triggers a sovereign default in your model. Let me walk through every piece.

---

## The economic story in one sentence

> **"The government defaults whenever its tax revenue falls short of its outstanding debt."**

That's it. The whole inequality is just a balance-sheet check at t=2: did the government collect enough taxes to pay back what it owes?

---

## Each symbol, defined

### Left side: `R·τ·x*` — what the government collects in taxes

This is **government tax revenue at t=2.**

- **`x*`** = the fraction of the bank's portfolio invested in long-term investment (per Lecture 11, x* ≈ 0.8 for US calibration)
- **`R`** = the gross return on investment if held to maturity (R > 1, e.g., R = 1.10 for a 10% return)
- **`τ`** = the tax rate the government charges on investment returns (Greek "tau," e.g., τ = 0.20 means 20% tax)

So `R·τ·x*` is read as: *"Investment return R, applied to fraction x* of the portfolio, taxed at rate τ."*

**Numerical example:** If R = 1.10, τ = 0.20, x* = 0.80:
`R·τ·x* = 1.10 × 0.20 × 0.80 = 0.176`

In words: the government collects **17.6 cents of tax revenue** per dollar deposited at t=0, paid at t=2 when investments mature.

### Right side: `D₀ + g` — what the government owes

This is **total outstanding government debt at t=2** that needs to be repaid.

- **`D₀`** = the government's *baseline* debt (existing borrowing, unrelated to the deposit insurance fund)
- **`g`** = the size of the deposit insurance fund — which in Izumi (2020) is *funded by issuing more government bonds*

So `D₀ + g` = *"Pre-existing debt plus the new debt the government issued to fund the DI fund."*

**Numerical example:** If D₀ = 1.20 (i.e., 120% of GDP, current US level) and g = $1.44T scaled per dollar = 0.08:
`D₀ + g = 1.20 + 0.08 = 1.28`

The government needs to repay **$1.28 per dollar of deposits** at t=2.

### The inequality `<` — when default happens

Default occurs **if** tax revenue is less than outstanding debt:

`0.176 < 1.28` → **default!**

Tax revenue can't cover what's owed → sovereign defaults.

---

## Why this creates the diabolic loop

This is the elegant part. Look at how the variables connect:

**Channel 1 — Tax revenue depends on bank investment.**
- If banks liquidate during a run, investment shrinks → x* falls → tax revenue `R·τ·x*` falls
- A banking crisis literally reduces the government's tax base

**Channel 2 — Government debt grows when banks fail.**
- If banks need DI payouts, the government issues more bonds → g rises → `D₀ + g` rises
- A banking crisis literally increases the government's debt obligations

**Both channels push toward default:**
- Crisis → x* falls (less tax) AND g rises (more debt)
- LHS shrinks, RHS grows
- Inequality flips toward `<` → default

This is why Izumi calls it a **twin-crisis equilibrium**: the banking crisis and the sovereign crisis are *the same thing happening from two sides.*

---

## Why your DepositCoin proposal breaks this

Recall: in your extension, the DI fund is split into `g = g_dom + K`, where `K` comes from foreign retail capital (NOT from issuing government bonds).

So the default condition becomes:

**Standard Izumi (2020):**
`R·τ·x* < D₀ + g_dom + K_treasury`

**Your model (DepositCoin):**
`R·τ·x* < D₀ + g_dom`

**Notice what disappeared from the right side:** `K`. Because foreign capital isn't government debt, it doesn't show up in `D₀ + g_dom`.

Mathematically, the right side is **smaller** in your version → the inequality is **less likely to flip to default** → the diabolic loop is weakened.

**For some range of parameters where the standard model defaults, your model doesn't.** That parameter range *is* your paper's preliminary result.

---

| Model | Default if... | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Izumi (2020) baseline** | `R·τ·x* < D₀ + g` | DI fund g is Treasury debt |

| **Difference** | RHS shrinks by exactly K | Foreign capital absorbs what would have been Treasury exposure |

The smaller the right side, the harder it is to default. By moving K *off* the government balance sheet and *onto* the foreign-retail balance sheet, you've shrunk the default trigger by exactly the size of the foreign-funded pool.
