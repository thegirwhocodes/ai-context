## Claude · 2026-04-21T02:38:23.203Z

For CRRA utility **u(c) = c^(1−γ)/(1−γ)** (with γ > 0, γ ≠ 1), the optimal Diamond-Dybvig contract is:

**c1\* = 1 / [π + (1−π) · R^((1−γ)/γ)]**

**c2\* = R^(1/γ) / [π + (1−π) · R^((1−γ)/γ)]**

(Same denominator for both.)

**Where it comes from:**
- FOC from the planner's problem: u'(c1) = R · u'(c2)
- With CRRA, u'(c) = c^(−γ), so c1^(−γ) = R · c2^(−γ) ⟹ **c2\*/c1\* = R^(1/γ)**
- Plug that ratio into the budget constraint π·c1 + (1−π)·c2/R = 1 and solve for c1*

**Special cases to sanity-check:**
- **γ = 1 (log utility):** denominator becomes π + (1−π) = 1, giving c1\* = 1 and c2\* = R ✓
- **γ > 1:** c1\* > 1, so bank is illiquid (not enough storage to pay everyone c1* even in normal times)
- The ratio c2\*/c1\* = R^(1/γ) tells you the "insurance": higher γ (more risk averse) ⟹ ratio closer to 1 ⟹ more consumption smoothing.

Source: [Econ 333 Cheat Sheet.md](Econ 333 - Financial Intermediation/Econ 333 Cheat Sheet.md) (Key Formulas table, line 90).

## Claude · 2026-04-21T04:00:22.796Z

**γ is the coefficient of risk aversion** — higher γ = more risk averse.

- **γ > 0:** risk averse (concave utility)
- **γ = 0:** risk neutral (linear utility)
- **γ = 1:** log utility (a common benchmark, moderately risk averse)
- **γ → ∞:** extremely risk averse (wants near-perfect consumption smoothing)

**Intuition for the D-D contract:**
The ratio c2\*/c1\* = R^(1/γ).
- When γ is **small** (less risk averse), 1/γ is large ⟹ big gap between c2\* and c1\* ⟹ investor is willing to tolerate big consumption differences to chase the higher return R.
- When γ is **large** (more risk averse), 1/γ is small ⟹ c2\* and c1\* are close together ⟹ investor wants smooth consumption across states, even if it means giving up return.

So γ measures how much the investor dislikes risk / consumption variability. Risk-loving would be γ < 0, which isn't used in this model.

Good questions — both get at something subtle about the setup.

## Q1: Why can't they just use Bank 1?

Because **Bank 1 is a narrow bank** — by definition, it is **legally restricted to holding only storage**. It cannot invest in the long-term technology at all. That's the whole point of narrow banking as a policy proposal: keep "safe" banks separate from investment activities.

So if depositors want any exposure to the long-term technology (which pays R > 1 at t = 2), they have no choice but to go through Bank 2. Bank 1 can only offer 1-to-1 storage returns.

Also, the problem **stipulates** a strictly positive amount in each bank — it's a constraint of the exercise, not something the depositor chooses freely. The point of the question is to compare this constrained two-bank setup to the unconstrained single D-D bank.

## Q2: Why does Bank 2 hold any storage?

Bank 2 still operates as a standard D-D bank facing **its own impatient depositors** at t = 1. Even though some liquidity is sitting at Bank 1, Bank 2 doesn't have access to it — each bank manages its own balance sheet.

So Bank 2 faces the classic D-D tradeoff:
- If it holds **all** deposits in long-term investment, it has to **liquidate at the penalty rate r < 1** whenever impatient depositors come to withdraw at t = 1. That's wasteful.
- If it holds some storage, it can meet t = 1 withdrawals without liquidating, preserving the full R value of long-term investment for t = 2.

Holding storage isn't about **preventing** runs — runs can still happen. It's about efficiently serving the **normal flow** of π impatient depositors (who always exist in equilibrium). The susceptibility to runs is a separate issue.

## The bigger picture (for parts 5 & 6)

- **Bank 1** is run-proof because it always has 1-to-1 storage backing every deposit — no maturity mismatch.
- **Bank 2** still has maturity mismatch (borrows short, invests long), so it's still susceptible to runs.
- **Outcome vs. original D-D:** worse, because forcing deposits into Bank 1 wastes the pooling/insurance benefit. The single D-D bank could do everything Bank 1 does AND offer c1\* > 1 through consumption smoothing. Splitting depositors across two banks shrinks the pool that gets that smoothing.

That's the narrow-banking tradeoff in part 6: **stability (no runs on Bank 1) comes at the cost of less maturity transformation** (less long-term investment funded, lower c1\*, lower welfare).

## Claude · 2026-04-21T17:00:52.341Z

Good question — and surprisingly, the answer is **no, c2 stays at R regardless of how many people withdraw early**. Let me show you why.

**Setup:** Bank 2 has 1 unit invested. Suppose fraction q of depositors withdraw at t = 1.

- **Cash needed at t = 1:** q · r (each of the q withdrawers gets r)
- **Investment liquidated:** q units (since liquidating 1 unit yields r, you need q units liquidated to raise q·r)
- **Investment remaining at t = 2:** (1 − q) units, which grows to **R·(1 − q)**
- **Patient depositors remaining:** (1 − q)
- **Per patient depositor:** R·(1 − q) / (1 − q) = **R**

**Why c2 doesn't drop:**

The penalty **r exactly matches the liquidation value**. That means each early withdrawer takes exactly "their share" of the investment — no more, no less. There's no destruction of value spilling over onto patient depositors.

Contrast this with a full D-D bank that promises c1\* > r: every early withdrawal destroys (c1\* − r)/r units of extra investment value. That loss gets dumped on the patient depositors at t = 2, which is how c2 falls.

**The key insight:**

Bank 2 in the narrow banking setup is basically giving each depositor **their own account** — they can liquidate their unit early for r, or hold for R. Nobody else's choice affects their payoff. So Bank 2 is actually **not susceptible to self-fulfilling runs** in this setup. No matter what you expect other depositors to do, waiting dominates (since R > r always).

This is a subtle but important property: **the r < 1 penalty is what makes Bank 2 run-proof**, because it aligns the early-withdrawal payoff with the liquidation value. If Bank 2 tried to promise c1\* > r like a D-D bank, then it'd be run-susceptible again — because then patient depositors would lose c2 value from each early withdrawal.

So to answer your question: c2 doesn't drop below 1 from early withdrawals in this setup. That's essentially Friedman's pitch for narrow banking — you lose the consumption-smoothing benefit (since early withdrawers get r < 1 instead of c1\*), but you gain run-proofness.

Right — so the depositor's optimization problem is: **choose y** (fraction to Bank 1) to maximize expected utility, given they'll be impatient with probability π and patient with probability (1−π).

**Payoffs for a given allocation y:**

- If impatient (withdraw at t = 1): consumes y + r·(1−y) = **r + y(1−r)**
- If patient (wait until t = 2): consumes y + R·(1−y) = **R − y(R−1)**

(Bank 1 storage is available at both t = 1 and t = 2 at 1-to-1, so patient depositors just let their Bank 1 balance sit.)

**Depositor solves:**

max over y: π · u(r + y(1−r)) + (1−π) · u(R − y(R−1))

**For log utility, the FOC gives:**

y\* = [π(1−r)R − (1−π)(R−1)r] / [(R−1)(1−r)]

**Intuition for the tradeoff:**

- **More in Bank 1 (higher y)** when: π is high (likely impatient), r is very low (big penalty for early Bank 2 withdrawal), very risk averse
- **More in Bank 2 (lower y)** when: R is high (big upside), r is close to 1 (small penalty), likely to be patient

**Quick sanity check** with π = 1/2, r = 1/2, R = 2:
y\* = [(1/2)(1/2)(2) − (1/2)(1)(1/2)] / [(1)(1/2)] = (1/4) / (1/2) = **1/2**

So they put half in each — symmetric given symmetric parameters. Makes sense.

**Why this is worse than single-bank D-D:**

The depositor is stuck on a **linear menu**: as y varies, (c1, c2) traces out a straight line between (r, R) and (1, 1). In a single D-D bank, the bank's contract gives a **curved** allocation that's strictly better for any risk-averse depositor — that's the value of pooling. The narrow banking split throws that away.

That's the welfare cost of narrow banking in part 6: safe, but you lose the curve.
