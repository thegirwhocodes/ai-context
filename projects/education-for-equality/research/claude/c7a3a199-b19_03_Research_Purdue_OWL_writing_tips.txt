## Writing Best Practices: A Practical Rules Reference

### PART 1: PURDUE OWL GUIDANCE

---

#### A. Clear, Concise Writing

**Core principle:** Concise writing does not always have the fewest words, but it always uses the strongest ones.

1. **Use specific, powerful words.** Several vague words can usually be replaced by one precise word.
2. **Check every word.** If a word adds nothing important or unique to a sentence, delete it or replace it.
3. **Use active voice by default.** Active sentences are shorter and clearer.
4. **Eliminate redundancy.** Cut words or phrases that seem to modify meaning but actually add nothing.
5. **Combine sentences.** If information does not require its own full sentence, fold it into an adjacent sentence.
6. **Front-load the important information.** Highlight the most important points early and succinctly.

#### B. Paragraph Structure and Topic Sentences

**Basic rule:** One idea per paragraph. When you transition to a new idea, start a new paragraph.

Every paragraph needs four elements:

1. **Unity** -- Begin and end on the same main point. Do not wander.
2. **Coherence** -- Use logical bridges (carrying one idea across sentences, parallel structure) and verbal bridges (repeating key words, synonyms, pronouns, transitions).
3. **A topic sentence** -- A general statement of the paragraph's main idea, usually placed first (or second, after a transition). Refine these during revision, not just during drafting.
4. **Adequate development** -- Go beyond 2-3 sentences. Use examples, data, quotations, anecdotes, definitions, comparisons, cause/effect, or description.

**The TTEB method for body paragraphs:**
- **T**ransition -- Smooth link from the previous paragraph
- **T**opic sentence -- States what this paragraph will discuss
- **E**vidence and analysis -- Specific support with deeper detail
- **B**rief wrap-up -- Tells the reader how/why this supports the thesis

**When to start a new paragraph:**
- Beginning a new idea or point
- Contrasting information
- When the reader needs a pause (long or complex material)
- Ending an introduction or starting a conclusion

#### C. Eliminating Wordiness

**Technique 1: Remove obvious or excessive detail.** Cut anything the reader already understands or can infer.

**Technique 2: Eliminate unnecessary determiners and modifiers.** Cut filler words:
- kind of, sort of, type of, really, basically, definitely, actually, generally, particular, individual, "for all intents and purposes"

**Technique 3: Cut repetitive wording.** If you have said the same thing twice in different words, keep the better version.

**Technique 4: Cut redundant pairs.** One word implies the other:
- "completely finish" --> "finish"
- "past memories" --> "memories"
- "unexpected surprise" --> "surprise"
- "basic fundamentals" --> "fundamentals"
- "very unique" --> "unique" (unique cannot be modified by degree)

**Technique 5: Cut redundant categories.** The specific word already implies the category:
- "large in size" --> "large"
- "pink in color" --> "pink"
- "period in time" --> "period"
- "economics field" --> "economics"

**Technique 6: Convert phrases to single words.**
- "the employee with ambition" --> "the ambitious employee"
- "the report, which was released recently" --> "the recently released report"
- "all applicants who are interested in the job" --> "all job applicants"

**Technique 7: Fix common pitfalls:**
- Kill expletive constructions: "It is the governor who signs bills" --> "The governor signs bills"
- Kill nominalizations: "The function of this department is the collection of accounts" --> "This department collects accounts"
- Kill unnecessary infinitive phrases: "The duty of a clerk is to check all incoming mail" --> "A clerk checks all incoming mail"
- Kill circumlocutions: "It is possible that nothing will come of these preparations" --> "Nothing may come of these preparations"

**The Paramedic Method** (Richard Lanham, via Purdue OWL) -- a 7-step revision process:
1. Circle the prepositions (of, in, about, for, onto, into)
2. Box the "is" verb forms
3. Ask "Where is the action?"
4. Convert the action into a simple verb
5. Move the doer into the subject position
6. Eliminate slow wind-ups (unnecessary introductory phrases)
7. Remove redundancies

Example: "In this paragraph is a demonstration of the use of good style in the writing of a report" (19 words) --> "This paragraph demonstrates good style in reports" (7 words)

#### D. Active Voice vs. Passive Voice

**Default rule: Use active voice.** The subject performs the action.
- Active: "Mrs. Simms opened an account" (5 words)
- Passive: "An account was opened by Mrs. Simms" (7 words)

**When passive voice is appropriate:**
- The agent is obvious, unimportant, or unknown
- You want to emphasize the action or the thing acted upon, not who did it
- Scientific writing (to create objective, fact-based tone)

#### E. Essay Structure, Flow, and Transitions

**Structure:**
- First paragraph: set context, explain why the topic matters, present a clear thesis statement
- Body paragraphs: each must logically connect to the thesis
- Conclusion: synthesize (do not merely repeat)

**Transitions:**
- Good transitions wrap up the idea from the previous section AND introduce the idea that follows
- Write transitions that could only connect *these specific* paragraphs -- not generic connectors that could link anything to anything
- Pick up key phrases from the previous paragraph to create continuity

**Flow (sentence-level, paragraph-level, text-level):**
- Move from old (known) information to new information
- Use transitional words to signal relationships (however, therefore, in addition, moreover, but, also)
- Place extra phrases/clauses at the beginning or end of sentences, not between subject and verb
- Use parallel constructions for series of related items
- Avoid noun strings
- Eliminate multiple negatives -- use affirmative constructions
- Choose action verbs over forms of "to be"
- Clarify every pronoun reference -- make sure "this," "that," "it," and "they" clearly point to something

#### F. Persuasive and Argumentative Writing

**Essay structure:**
- Para 1: set context, explain importance, state thesis
- Body: each paragraph directly supports thesis with evidence; explain *how and why* the evidence supports your claim
- Counterargument: dedicate 1-2 paragraphs to opposing views and explain why your position is stronger
- Conclusion: synthesize

**Three rhetorical appeals (use all three):**

1. **Logos (logic):** Use inductive reasoning (specific evidence --> generalization) and deductive reasoning (generalization --> specific application). Avoid fallacies: slippery slope, hasty generalization, false cause, circular argument, either/or, ad hominem, red herring.

2. **Ethos (credibility):** Use credible sources and cite them properly. Represent opposing views accurately. Establish shared values with your audience. Disclose personal interests. Organize logically. Proofread.

3. **Pathos (emotion):** Use individual stories and interviews to "give a human face to the numbers." Emotional appeals must genuinely support your claims, not distract from substance or misrepresent the topic.

### PART 2: CLASSIC STYLE GUIDES

---

#### G. George Orwell's Six Rules ("Politics and the English Language," 1946)

1. **Never use a metaphor, simile, or figure of speech you are used to seeing in print.** (Avoid cliches.)
2. **Never use a long word where a short one will do.**
3. **If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.**
4. **Never use the passive where you can use the active.**
5. **Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word, or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent.**
6. **Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.** (Rules serve clarity; if following a rule makes the sentence worse, break it.)

Additional Orwell principle: "Let the meaning choose the word, and not the other way about." Think clearly first using mental images, then select language -- do not let prefabricated phrases dictate your meaning.

#### H. Strunk & White's *Elements of Style* -- 11 Principles of Composition

1. **Choose a suitable design and hold to it.** Plan your structure before writing.
2. **Make the paragraph the unit of composition.** Build around well-structured paragraphs.
3. **Use the active voice.**
4. **Put statements in positive form.** Say what something *is*, not what it *is not*. ("He usually came late" not "He was not very often on time.")
5. **Use definite, specific, concrete language.** Avoid vague abstractions.
6. **Omit needless words.** "Vigorous writing is concise. A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, for the same reason that a drawing should have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts."
7. **Avoid a succession of loose sentences.** Vary sentence structure.
8. **Express coordinate ideas in similar form.** (Parallel structure.)
9. **Keep related words together.**
10. **In summaries, keep to one tense.**
11. **Place the emphatic words of a sentence at the end.** The end of a sentence carries the most weight.

#### I. Strunk & White -- 21 Style Reminders

1. Place yourself in the background
2. Write in a way that comes naturally
3. Work from a suitable design
4. **Write with nouns and verbs** (not adjectives and adverbs)
5. **Revise and rewrite**
6. **Do not overwrite**
7. **Do not overstate**
8. **Avoid the use of qualifiers** (rather, very, little, pretty -- "these are the leeches that infest the pond of prose")
9. Do not affect a breezy manner
10. Use orthodox spelling
11. **Do not explain too much** -- trust the reader
12. Do not construct awkward adverbs
13. Make sure the reader knows who is speaking
14. **Avoid fancy words** -- prefer the plain word
15. Do not use dialect unless your ear is good
16. **Be clear** -- clarity is the priority
17. Do not inject opinion (in expository writing)
18. Use figures of speech sparingly
19. Do not take shortcuts at the cost of clarity
20. Avoid foreign languages
21. Prefer the standard to the offbeat

#### J. William Zinsser's *On Writing Well* -- Four Commandments

1. **Clarity** -- Guide the reader through your thought process logically.
2. **Simplicity** -- Strip every sentence to its cleanest components. Remove words with no function. Replace long words with shorter ones. Remove adverbs whose meaning is already in the verb.
3. **Brevity** -- Say it once, say it well, move on.
4. **Humanity** -- Be yourself. "There is no style store; style is organic to the person doing the writing."

Additional Zinsser principles:
- "Clear thinking becomes clear writing; one can't exist without the other."
- Rewriting is the key to good writing. No sentence is both concise and complete without being rewritten.
- Unity of tense, mood, pronoun, and tone throughout a piece.
