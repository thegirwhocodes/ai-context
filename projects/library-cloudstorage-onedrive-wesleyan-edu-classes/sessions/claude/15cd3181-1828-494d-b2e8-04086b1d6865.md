## AD-AS, From Scratch

### Step 0 — What's on the axes (don't skip this, it's where most people get confused)

In Imai's class:
- **Y axis (vertical)** = inflation rate (π) — like 2%, 5%, etc.
- **X axis (horizontal)** = real output Y (think real GDP)

NOT price level vs. output. It's **inflation** vs. output. This matters because the curves move differently than what you might've seen in 302.

---

### The three curves at a glance

```
π  |     LRAS (vertical line at Y*)
   |       |
   |       |   SRAS (upward slope)
   |       |  /
   |       | /
   |       |/
   |       *  ← long-run equilibrium
   |      /|
   |     / |
   |    /  |
   |  AD (downward slope)
   +-------+--------> Y
           Y*
```

- **AD** — downward sloping. Shows how total spending in the economy responds to inflation.
- **SRAS** — upward sloping. Shows how firms' production responds to inflation in the short run.
- **LRAS** — vertical at potential output Y\*. Shows that in the long run, output is determined by the economy's productive capacity, not by inflation.

The economy sits where AD and SRAS cross. Long-run equilibrium = where all three cross at once.

---

### Why AD slopes DOWN — the mechanism

Plain version: when inflation is higher, real output ends up lower. But WHY?

**The chain (memorize this — it's exam gold):**

```
π ↑  →  Fed raises r (MP rule)  →  borrowing costs ↑  →  I and C ↓  →  Y ↓
```

Step by step:

1. **Inflation rises** above the Fed's target.
2. **The Fed responds** with the MP rule: r = r̄ + φπ. Higher inflation → higher real interest rate. (This is by ASSUMPTION — it's the Taylor Principle, the Fed's policy rule.)
3. **Higher r kills demand:**
   - Investment ↓ — firms borrow less because projects aren't profitable anymore
   - Consumption ↓ — saving more attractive, also borrowing for cars/houses more expensive
   - Net exports ↓ — higher r attracts foreign capital → currency appreciates → exports fall
4. **Lower spending = lower output.** Y falls.

So AD slopes down ONLY because the Fed is doing its job. That's the key insight Imai cares about.

**What happens if Fed violates the Taylor Principle?** AD slopes UP (unstable). Higher inflation doesn't get fought → real rates fall → demand stimulated → inflation rises more → spiral. This is what happened in the 1970s.

**What shifts AD?** Anything that changes spending at a given inflation level:
- G ↑ (gov spending) → AD right
- T ↑ (taxes) → AD left
- Stock market boom (wealth ↑) → AD right
- Currency appreciates → AD left
- Fed becomes more hawkish (MP shifts up) → AD left

---

### Why SRAS slopes UP — the mechanism

Plain version: when output is higher, inflation is higher. But WHY?

This comes from gluing two pieces together: **the Phillips curve + Okun's law.**

**Okun's law:** higher output ↔ lower unemployment.
- When firms produce more, they hire more workers. So Y ↑ ⇒ u ↓.

**Phillips curve:** lower unemployment ↔ higher inflation (in the short run).
- When unemployment is low (tight labor market), workers can demand higher wages. Firms pay them, then pass costs through as higher prices. So u ↓ ⇒ π ↑.

**Glue them together:**
```
Y ↑  →  u ↓  →  wages and costs ↑  →  π ↑
```

That's why SRAS slopes up. The deep mechanism is **labor market tightness.**

**What shifts SRAS?**

- **Expected inflation (πᵉ) ↑:** Workers and firms bake higher inflation into wage contracts and pricing. SRAS shifts UP.
- **Adverse supply shock** (oil price spike, bad harvest, productivity collapse): production costs ↑ → firms charge more at every output level → SRAS UP.
- **Favorable supply shock**: SRAS DOWN.

---

### Why LRAS is VERTICAL

In the long run, output is determined by **real things** — your factories, workers, technology, capital stock — not by how much paper money is floating around.

Think of it this way: doubling all the prices (and all the wages) doesn't change how many cars Toyota can build. Real productive capacity doesn't depend on inflation.

So LRAS is a vertical line at **potential output Y\***.

**What shifts LRAS?**
- More capital, more workers, better technology → LRAS RIGHT
- Capital destroyed (war, pandemic), labor shortage → LRAS LEFT

---

### The Self-Correcting Mechanism (THE engine of the model)

This is the most important thing to understand. The economy is self-correcting because **expectations adjust over time.**

**Case 1: Y > Y\* (overheating, like a boom)**

```
Y > Y*
  ↓
Labor markets tight, unemployment below natural rate
  ↓
Wages rise, costs rise, firms charge more
  ↓
Inflation rises ABOVE what people expected
  ↓
People say "oh, inflation is higher than I thought" → πᵉ rises
  ↓
Workers demand even higher wages going forward → SRAS shifts UP
  ↓
This reduces output back toward Y*
  ↓
Eventually: Y returns to Y*, but at a HIGHER inflation rate forever
```

**Case 2: Y < Y\* (slack, like a recession)**

Same logic in reverse:
```
Y < Y* → loose labor market → wages stagnate → π falls below expected
  → πᵉ falls → SRAS shifts DOWN → Y rises back to Y*
  → Final: Y = Y* again, but at LOWER inflation
```

**The key point:** the economy ALWAYS returns to Y\* in the long run. What changes permanently is the **inflation rate**, not the output.

---

### AD shocks vs AS shocks (the most-tested distinction)

**Positive AD shock (e.g., big tax cut):**

```
Step 1 (short run):     AD shifts RIGHT
                        New equilibrium: Y ↑, π ↑
                        (Both go up)

Step 2 (over time):     Y > Y* → πᵉ rises → SRAS shifts UP

Step 3 (long run):      Y returns to Y*
                        But π is permanently HIGHER
```

So a demand boost gives you a **temporary** burst of growth and a **permanent** rise in inflation. That's the bargain.

**Negative AS shock (e.g., oil shock):**

```
Step 1 (short run):     SRAS shifts UP (left)
                        New equilibrium: Y ↓, π ↑
                        This is STAGFLATION — both bad at once

Step 2 (over time):     If shock is temporary, SRAS shifts back down
                        Y returns to Y*, π returns to original
```

**Why is AS shock special?** It creates a **policy dilemma**. If the Fed tightens to fight inflation, it makes the recession worse. If the Fed eases to fight the recession, it makes inflation worse. This is the **breakdown of the divine coincidence**. There's no free lunch.

For demand shocks, fighting inflation also fixes output (no tradeoff). For supply shocks, you have to pick one.

---

### A worked example you'll be tested on

**Scenario:** Consumption suddenly drops (people get scared about a recession).

1. **AD shifts left** (lower spending at every π).
2. **Short run:** New AD ∩ SRAS gives lower Y (recession) AND lower π.
3. **Self-correcting mechanism kicks in:** Y < Y\* → labor market slack → πᵉ falls → SRAS shifts DOWN.
4. **Long run:** Y returns to Y\*, π is permanently LOWER.
5. **What should the Fed do?** **Lower rates** — shift AD right to offset the original drop. This avoids the recession entirely.

If you can walk through that chain in 3-4 sentences in an essay, you'll nail any AD-AS exam question.

---

### One more piece: the ZLB (zero lower bound)

If π drops enough that the Fed wants to set i below 0% but can't (you can't pay people to take loans):
- AD curve becomes UPWARD-sloping at very low π (because deflation actually RAISES real rates when nominal rates are stuck at zero)
- The self-correcting mechanism BREAKS
- Falling π makes the recession WORSE, not better
- This is the **deflationary spiral / liquidity trap** — Japan in the 90s, the US in 2008–09
- Cure: fiscal policy, QE, forward guidance — monetary policy alone can't fix it

---

**TL;DR (pin this in your head):**

| Curve | Slope | Why |
|---|---|---|
| AD | DOWN | π ↑ → Fed raises r → spending ↓ → Y ↓ |
| SRAS | UP | Y ↑ → u ↓ → wages ↑ → π ↑ |
| LRAS | VERTICAL | Real capacity doesn't depend on π |

| Shock | Short run | Long run |
|---|---|---|
| +AD | Y ↑, π ↑ | Y back to Y\*, π permanently higher |
| −AD | Y ↓, π ↓ | Y back to Y\*, π permanently lower |
| −AS | Y ↓, π ↑ (stagflation) | Y back to Y\*, π back to start |
| +AS | Y ↑, π ↓ | Y back to Y\*, π back to start |

Engine of long-run adjustment: **expectations adjust → SRAS shifts → Y returns to Y\***.
