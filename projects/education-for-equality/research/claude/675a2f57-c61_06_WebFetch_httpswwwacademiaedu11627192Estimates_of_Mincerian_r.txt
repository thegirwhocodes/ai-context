# Mincerian Returns to Schooling in Nigeria - Complete Content

## Article Metadata
**Title:** Estimates of Mincerian returns to schooling in Nigeria
**Author:** Adebayo B. Aromolaran
**Published:** Oxford Development Studies, Vol. 34, No. 2, June 2006
**DOI:** 10.1080/13600810600707433
**Data Period:** 1996/97-1998/99

---

## Abstract Summary

The research investigates private returns to education across different attainment levels. Key findings indicate returns vary substantially by education stage: "small at primary and secondary levels, 2-3% and 4%, respectively, but substantial at post-secondary education level, 10-15%."

The study uses General Household Survey data and concludes that expanding basic education cannot be justified on efficiency grounds alone, though post-secondary returns are sufficient to justify student tuition policies.

---

## Methodology

### Theoretical Framework
The analysis employs human capital investment theory, deriving earnings functions from Mincer's model. The specification assumes individuals maximize discounted present value of lifetime earnings given schooling choices.

### Data Source
**Nigerian General Household Survey (GHS)** - three years of quarterly data:
- 1996/97: 24,889 households
- 1997/98: 32,024 households
- 1998/99: 27,649 households
- Total: 375,399 individuals

**Sample construction:**
- Initial working-age population (15-64): 205,135
- Final analytical sample: 93,999 individuals
  - 9,065 male wage earners
  - 3,135 female wage earners
  - 55,135 male self-employed
  - 24,826 female self-employed

Exclusions: zero work hours, zero income, working students, extreme hours (>90/week), inappropriate schooling data, outlier wages (>100 naira/hour).

### Econometric Models

**Model 1 - Constant Returns Specification:**
ln w = α + βS + δ₁E + δ₂E² + λ₁(1997) + λ₂(1998) + μ

**Model 2 - Three-Segment Spline:**
ln w = α + β₁Pry + β₂Sec + β₃Postsec + δ₁E + δ₂E² + λ₁(1997) + λ₂(1998) + μ

**Model 3 - Age-Interaction Spline:**
ln w = α + β₁Pry + β₂Sec + β₃Postsec + δ₁E + δ₂E² + λ₁(1997) + λ₂(1998) + μ
(with separate coefficients by age cohort: 25-34, 35-44, 45-64)

**Variable Definitions:**
- ln w: natural log of real hourly earnings (deflated to 1995 base)
- S: total years of schooling (0-17)
- Pry: years of primary schooling (0-6)
- Sec: years of secondary schooling (0-6)
- Postsec: years of post-secondary schooling (0-5)
- E: potential working experience (age - years schooling - 6)
- E²: quadratic experience term (×10⁻²)

## Historical Comparison

**Psacharopoulos (1973) - 1966 Data from Western Nigeria:**
- Primary: 30% returns
- Secondary: 14% returns
- Tertiary: 34% returns

**Current Study (1996-99):**
- Primary: 2-3% returns
- Secondary: 4% returns
- Post-secondary: 10-15% returns

**Decline:** Current estimates show 83-90% reduction compared to 1966 estimates.

## Key Findings

1. **Convex Returns Pattern:** "returns to schooling are convex" - increasing at higher education levels rather than following diminishing returns principle.

2. **Gender Disparities in Wage Levels:** While women receive higher per-year returns, male wage earners earn 11-13% more overall; self-employed females earn 24-33% less.

3. **Sectoral Differences:** Wage workers receive higher returns than self-employed across all education levels, suggesting better market compensation for credentials in formal employment.

4. **Quality Decline:** The precipitous fall from 1966 estimates attributed to "substantial decline in school quality and significant increases in the supply of educated workers between 1976 and 1999."

5. **Universal Primary Education Impact:** UPE program (1976-1981) increased supply of primary-educated workers, contributing to declining primary education returns among younger cohorts.

---

## Policy Implications Discussed

The author argues that:

- Public investment in basic education cannot be justified on efficiency grounds given low returns (2-4%), unless quality improvements are implemented or equity redistributions are primary goals.

- "Students at this level should pay tuition to recoup more of the public costs of schooling" for post-secondary education, given high private returns (10-15%).

- Quality improvements in basic education would enhance returns and justify public investment more effectively.

- Scholarship and loan programs for low-income students at post-secondary level would improve equity while maintaining efficiency gains.

## Statistical Testing

**F-test Results (Model Specification):**
The hypothesis of constant returns across education levels was rejected at 5% significance level, supporting the three-segment spline specification over the single coefficient model.

**Age-Education Interactions:**
Post-secondary education × age cohort interaction was statistically significant at 5% level for both males and females. Primary and secondary interactions were not statistically significant at 5% level for most comparisons (see Appendix 2).

---

## Data Quality Notes

- Income variable: Monthly earnings from all jobs including allowances
- Schooling: Measured by level attained and highest grade completed
- Real wages: Deflated using consumer price index (1995 base)
- Hourly rates: Monthly income ÷ monthly work hours
- No controls included for: Geographic location, occupation, sector (to capture full schooling effects)

---

## Limitations Acknowledged

1. **Omitted Variable Bias:** Unmeasured ability, family background, health not controlled; estimated bias <10% of coefficients (per Card 2001).

2. **Sample Selection Bias:** Not corrected due to data limitations; wage sector non-randomly selected. However, studies in sub-Saharan Africa show OLS estimates remain robust after correction.

3. **Self-Employment Measurement Issues:** Self-employment earnings include returns to physical capital and entrepreneurial risk, not just human capital returns.

4. **Missing Exclusion Restrictions:** Unable to implement instrumental variable approach; lacks suitable natural experiments in GHS data.

---

## Conclusion Statement

The author concludes that observed low returns to primary/secondary education and high returns to post-secondary education explain Nigeria's recent enrollment patterns: "low and/or declining private returns to primary and secondary education" alongside "high and/or increasing rate of return" at post-secondary levels account for declining basic enrollment and rising higher education enrollment since 1980.
