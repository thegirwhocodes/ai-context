Good question, and the answer is the reverse of what it feels like.

**Your phone has the OS running. It does not have the pieces needed to build for it or debug on it.** Three separate things are missing, and none of them ship to consumers.

**1. The SDK isn't the OS.** To compile, the compiler needs headers, stub libraries (`.tbd`), and module maps — a description of the *shape* of every iOS API. Your phone has compiled machine code, not headers. That's a build-time artifact Apple strips from shipping devices entirely.

**2. The Developer Disk Image is deliberately absent from your phone — for security.** This is the interesting part. `debugserver`, the Instruments daemons, the memory diagnostic tools: none of them are on a consumer iPhone. They're mounted temporarily by Xcode at the start of a debug session, into a RAM disk, then discarded.

Apple does it that way on purpose. A phone shipping with a live debug server would be a serious hole — anything that can attach a debugger to arbitrary processes can read their memory. So the tools live on the Mac and get lent to the phone for the duration of a session.

That's also why the version has to match exactly. The DDI is signed by Apple against your specific iOS build, and the device rejects one signed for a different build. Xcode 26.2 had no image signed for iOS 26.5, so the phone refused it — that's the error we hit.

**3. You couldn't copy it off the phone anyway.** iOS runs from a Signed System Volume — sealed and cryptographically verified. Nothing reads it out, and the pieces aren't in there to begin with.

**And why a download at all:** since Xcode 15, Apple unbundled platform components to shrink the base install. Xcode used to ship every SDK and simulator for every platform. Now it ships the toolchain and macOS bits, and you download only the platforms you actually target. Your fresh Xcode 26.6 has the compiler but no iOS platform until we fetch it.

So: **your Mac is lending your phone the debugger, not borrowing anything from it.**

## Chargebacks — the risk isn't lawsuits, it's losing Stripe

- **$15 per dispute**, win or lose. Since June 2025 a *lost* dispute costs **$30**.
- **Above 0.75%** dispute rate, Stripe treats you as high-risk → monitoring, reserves, or **account termination**.
- **Above 1% for two consecutive months** → monitoring program with an extra **$50 per dispute**.
- Stripe can warn or suspend you *before* you hit card-network thresholds.

Why your pattern generates them: a year later someone who forgot gets a $100 charge and disputes it with their bank instead of asking you for a refund. With 500 renewals, **four disputes puts you at 0.8%** — past the threshold. That's not a big number.

Losing your Stripe account when it's your only processor is the actual worst case, not a fine.

## Yes, Stripe Checkout shows it — and that isn't sufficient

Checkout does render "then $100.00 per year" for a discounted subscription. But two requirements sit outside that page:

**A renewal reminder is separately required.** California requires notice of the price change **7 to 30 days before** the new fee takes effect. Showing it at purchase a year earlier doesn't satisfy that. You have to send the email.

**And this one hits your churn ladder directly.** Since July 1, 2025, when you show a retention offer during cancellation, a **"click to cancel" button must be visible on screen simultaneously and stay visible the entire time the offer is displayed.**

So your flow — cancel → 30% off → cancel again → $19.99 — is legal **only if** each of those screens shows a working cancel button the whole time. What you can't do is make them click through two offers to reach cancellation. Same number of clicks as signup.

That's a design constraint on the exact feature you described, and it's cheap to build correctly now.

## Name in the email funnel: no

Email only. Every extra field costs conversion, you can't verify a name anyway, and the personalisation gain is marginal. If you ever want it, ask after they're already a user.
