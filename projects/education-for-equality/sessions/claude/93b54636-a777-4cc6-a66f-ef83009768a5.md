## Claude · 2026-08-20T19:32:08.786Z

Two things I checked before answering, because they decide the whole architecture:

**Real alarms are now possible for third-party apps — but only natively.** iOS 26's **AlarmKit** gives your app the same powers as Apple's Clock: alarms that fire through Silent mode and Focus, a full-screen stop/snooze display, Lock Screen + Dynamic Island + Apple Watch presence, and custom snooze lengths (1–15 min). Before iOS 26 this was impossible — alarm apps faked it with notifications that died on reboot. That means: **this app has to be a native iOS app.** A web app on Vercel physically cannot wake a locked phone. (You have an active Apple Developer account, so you're clear to ship.)

**Pinterest access is real but gated.** Pinterest API v5 is free, needs a Business account + OAuth, and gives Trial access in ~1–2 business days; Standard access needs a demo video and 1–4 weeks review. So Pinterest is viable, but it's a dependency I'd rather not have blocking your first working alarm.

Three honest flags on the concept before we build:

1. **Don't invent the statistics.** "X% of people who snoozed didn't reach their goals" isn't a real finding, and fabricated stats are the fastest way to lose a user's trust the moment they Google one. There *is* real research on snoozing and sleep inertia we can cite truthfully — and more powerfully, **her own data** ("you've snoozed 11 times in 3 days, 47 minutes gone this week") is real, personal, and hits harder than any borrowed statistic.
2. **The lock needs a pressure valve.** An alarm you genuinely cannot escape will get you rejected in App Store review and will scare users. The proven pattern is a **time-delayed lock**: you can change settings, but changes take effect in 24 hours — so you can't weaken it in the moment of weakness, which is the whole point.
3. **Your instinct is right and it has a name.** Escalating consequence framing beats brute force (math puzzles, shaking). That's the actual differentiator here.
