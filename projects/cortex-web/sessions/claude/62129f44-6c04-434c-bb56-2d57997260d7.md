## Claude · 2026-08-03T19:00:37.424Z

Sure. Here's the walk-through — the *why* behind each section, not just what's in the doc.

## The core insight shaping everything

Cortex isn't a normal beta. Two things make it different:

1. **The ask is huge.** You're asking people to connect Gmail — their most sensitive digital surface — to an app built by a solo founder. That's a trust ask most beta programs don't make.
2. **The value takes weeks to appear.** A LoRA needs ingested data to be worth anything. If someone signs up and leaves after 20 minutes, you learned nothing about whether Cortex works — only that your landing page didn't hook them.

Those two facts mean **quantity of testers is actively harmful** early on. 50 strangers who churn in a week teach you less than 5 people who live in the product for a month. The whole plan is built around that.

## Why three waves, not one beta

The temptation is to open a waitlist, pull 30 people, launch. Don't. Instead, **three concentric rings**:

- **Wave 1 (5–8 people, trust circle)** — your roommates, family, closest E4E collaborators. Their job is to find the showstopper bugs in a relationship where telling you the truth is cheap. If Cortex embarrasses you here, no lasting damage.
- **Wave 2 (15–25 people, warm ring)** — Wesleyan entrepreneurship, E4E donors, Christian-founder networks (Praxis, Ocean Network), church community. These people trust *you* but aren't personally invested — so you learn if Cortex works when Naomi's warmth isn't carrying it.
- **Wave 3 (30–60, cold-but-qualified)** — curated sub-communities, screened via a 5-question intake. This is where you learn if Cortex works without any of Naomi in the room.

Each wave has an explicit gate before opening the next. That protects you from scaling problems downward — you never end up with 40 people all needing your attention at once.

## Who to actually recruit

Not "productivity nerds" — that's the wrong ICP. The testers whose feedback is most valuable are:

- **Neurodivergent knowledge workers (ADHD especially)** — highest pain, most articulate, most distinctive personality signal for the LoRA. If Cortex works for them it works for anyone.
- **Solopreneurs, writers, therapists, coaches** — their email *is* their livelihood, and they have stylistically distinctive writing (great LoRA fodder).
- **Christian founder/ministry networks** — plays to your identity, high trust dividends, and these people usually juggle 3+ roles (ministry + job + family), which stresses the spheres feature.
- **Grad students / researchers** — inbox chaos + calendar Tetris + writing style, a perfect trifecta.

I called out an anti-profile too: **don't recruit lawyers, doctors, or anyone with legally privileged material** in their inbox. Not because Cortex would misuse it, but because the risk to *them* is real and it's the wrong first cohort to prove yourself on.

## Sourcing — where to actually find them

I ranked channels by (fit × trust) ÷ effort. The top tier is entirely warm:

1. Your personal network
2. Wesleyan orgs (WesTech, Patricelli, etc.)
3. E4E supporter list
4. Christian founder communities
5. Wave-1 referrals (each brings 2 hand-picked)

Deliberately **not** in the top tier: Product Hunt, BetaList, Twitter posts, Reddit. Those attract tourists — people who love beta-testing as a hobby but won't actually use the product. Save them for wave 4+ (public).

## Screening — 5 questions that filter 70% of noise

A short intake form does most of the work. The two most important questions:

- **"How comfortable are you connecting Gmail to an app built by a solo founder?"** — anything under 7/10 = auto-decline (with a warm "let's revisit"). Trust bar is the whole game.
- **"What would have to be true for a personal AI to be worth $19/month?"** — a qualitative filter. Thoughtful answers correlate perfectly with useful testers. One-word answers = decline.

## Onboarding — inversely proportional to wave

Wave 1 gets a live 45-min screen-share. Wave 2 gets group Zooms of 5–8. Wave 3 gets a Loom. **This is the difference between a beta that works and one that doesn't** — most beta failures are onboarding failures, not product failures.

## What we're actually measuring

Not vanity metrics. Each metric answers a question:

- **D7 / D30 retention** → is Cortex sticky enough that the LoRA gets data to learn from?
- **Gmail connect rate** → is the trust bar cleared?
- **Blind-tasted LoRA vs. Haiku preference** → is the whole "not just RAG" thesis actually true? This is the make-or-break number.
- **Actions approved & executed** → does the agentic layer earn trust?
- **Unprompted "I'd pay for this" mentions** → the only pricing signal that matters at this stage.

## The risks I flagged

The scariest ones:

- **Naomi burnout** — you can't personally onboard 30 people. The wave structure exists specifically to prevent this. Don't let anyone talk you into skipping it.
- **A bad email sent on someone's behalf** — never enable auto-send in waves 1–3. Every action goes through the approval flow. Say this explicitly during onboarding.
- **Testers churning silently** — every 14-day-inactive user gets a personal email from you asking for 3 sentences. No form. Human ask. This is where most beta programs go blind.
