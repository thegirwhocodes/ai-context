## Claude · 2026-05-04T02:12:07.534Z

In plain English:

**Cross-country panel** — line up many countries (e.g., 40 African countries) over many years and look at all the rows together. Lots of data points, but you're mixing very different countries.

**Time-series** — one country only (Nigeria), tracked year after year. Cleaner story but few data points (~30 years).

**States panel** — same idea as cross-country, but Nigeria's 36 states instead of countries. More data, harder to get.

**OLS (Ordinary Least Squares)** — the basic regression you learned in stats class. Draw the best-fit line through the data and read off the slopes.

**ARDL bounds testing** — a fancier regression. "Auto-Regressive Distributed Lag." It lets each variable affect the answer with a delay (last year's education affects this year's growth, etc.) and works even when your data has trends and you only have ~30 years. The "bounds test" is a yes/no check for whether the variables are actually moving together in the long run.

**FMOLS / DOLS** — alternative versions of OLS that are corrected for two annoying problems in time-series data (variables drifting together, and one variable causing another). What Eregha used in his Nigeria paper.

**Cointegration** — fancy word for "these variables drift together over the long run, even if they wiggle differently year to year." If education and GDP are cointegrated, you can confidently say one drives the other.

**Stationarity** — does the variable bounce around a stable average, or does it just keep climbing? GDP keeps climbing (non-stationary). The growth rate of GDP bounces around (stationary). ARDL needs you to know which is which.

**Error-correction model (ECM)** — the part of ARDL that says "if education and GDP got out of sync last year, here's how fast they snap back together this year."

**Oil-dependence interaction** — a regression term that asks: "does an extra year of school matter LESS in years when oil revenue is high?" That's your novel angle.

**Convergence** — Solow's prediction that poor countries should catch up to rich ones over time.

**PWT human capital index (`hc`)** — Penn World Tables' single number summarizing how educated a country's workforce is. A continuous variable from 1 to ~4. You're using this *instead of* the OOSC % because OOSC has the data-gap problem.

**OOSC robustness check** — for the few years where you DO have OOSC data, run the regression again and see if you get the same answer. If yes → confidence boost. If no → you have to explain why.

## ⚠️ The puzzle — `hc` long-run coefficient is **negative**

Long-run elasticities (computed as −β/φ from the ECM):

| Variable | Long-run coef | Expected sign | Verdict |
|---|---:|:---:|---|
| **`hc` (human capital)** | **−1.59** | + | ❌ **wrong sign** |
| Investment (`csh_i`) | −0.57 | + | ❌ wrong sign |
| Trade openness | −0.29 | + | ❌ wrong sign |
| Inflation | −0.07 | − | ✓ |
| Life expectancy | **+7.21** | + | ✓ huge |
| Population growth | +0.17 | − | ❌ wrong sign |
| Oil rents | +0.00 | ambiguous | flat |

`hc`, investment, and trade all loading the *wrong way*, while life expectancy is doing all the human-capital lifting. Not the result you publish.

## What's likely going on

1. **Severe multicollinearity** — condition number = **1.53 × 10⁴** (anything above ~30 is concerning). Life expectancy and `hc` are both monotonically rising over 1990–2023; they're absorbing each other's variance.
2. **The `hc` data caveat from the live research** — Nigeria's PWT `hc` is built solely from Cohen-Soto-Leker decadal observations and **linearly interpolated**. Annual variation is mechanical, not real. The ADF test even flagged it as I(2)? (which violates ARDL's I(0)/I(1) assumption).
3. **Overfit risk** — 31 obs, 15 parameters. The R² jump from 0.47 (basic OLS) to 0.82 (full ECM) is partly degrees-of-freedom inflation.

## Recommended next moves (pick one)

1. **Drop life expectancy**, keep `hc` as the headline education variable. Most likely fix — life_exp is collinear with `hc` and stealing its coefficient.
2. **Drop `hc`**, use life expectancy as the human-capital proxy. Defensible (Bloom-Canning-Sevilla), and life_exp has clean annual data not interpolation.
3. **Switch `hc` for `enroll_primary` (gross primary enrollment)**, which has real annual variation (29 obs).
4. **Trim the model to a parsimonious 4-5 regressor spec** to address overfitting — e.g., {`hc`, `csh_i`, `oil_rents`, `inflation`}.

Which direction? I'd start with **#1 (drop life expectancy)** — fastest test of whether collinearity is the real issue.
