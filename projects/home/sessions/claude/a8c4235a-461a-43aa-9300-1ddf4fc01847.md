## What "r" means
`r` is a **correlation coefficient** — a single number from -1 to +1 that says how tightly two things move together.
- **+1** = perfect lockstep (when A goes up, B goes up by the same amount)
- **0** = no relationship
- **-1** = perfect mirror (when A goes up, B goes down)
- Above **|0.7|** is considered "very strong" in social science

So when you present these numbers, you're saying *"our news-derived score moves with reality very tightly."*

---

# ICRG Inversion Explanation

## The original ICRG convention

The PRS Group's International Country Risk Guide (ICRG) — which we replicated — uses a **0–100 composite score where higher = SAFER**. Switzerland scores ~90, the US ~80, Nigeria ~40, Sudan ~20. The score is built up from three pillars: Political (max 50 points), Economic (max 25 points), Financial (max 25 points), all summed.

This is intuitive once you know it, but it trips audiences up because most people read a chart line going UP as "things are getting worse." With ICRG, a line going UP means things are getting BETTER.

## What we did

We kept the ICRG composite score in our pipeline as `composite_score` — every internal calculation, validation, and macro anchoring uses the original ICRG convention (higher = safer). We did NOT change the math.

For presentation only, we added a parallel column called `risk_index`, computed as:

```
risk_index = 100 − composite_score
```

That's the entire inversion. A one-line subtraction. No reweighting of factors, no recalibration of pillars, no re-running of the LLM. The information is identical — we're just flipping the axis so a line going UP means risk going UP, which matches audience intuition.

## Both versions live side by side

In every `outputs/<country>/10_quarterly_scores.csv`, you'll find both columns:

| quarter | composite_score | risk_index | political_score | economic_score | financial_score |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2023Q2 | 41.65 | 58.35 | 20.28 | 10.63 | 10.75 |

- `composite_score` = 41.65 → "High Risk" in ICRG terms (40–50 band)
- `risk_index` = 58.35 → "High Risk" in our inverted terms (50–60 band)

Same risk classification, opposite axis. The pillar columns (`political_score`, `economic_score`, `financial_score`) stay in ICRG convention because they're directly summed to produce `composite_score`.

## Risk bands flip exactly

| ICRG composite (higher = safer) | Our risk_index (higher = riskier) | Band |
|---|---|---|
| 80–100 | 0–20 | Very Low Risk |
| 70–80 | 20–30 | Low Risk |
| 60–70 | 30–40 | Moderate Risk |
| 50–60 | 40–50 | High Risk |
| 0–50 | 50–100 | **Very High Risk** |

The "Very High Risk floor at 50" line you see on every chart is the boundary: above 50 on `risk_index` (or below 50 on `composite_score`) = Very High Risk, which is where Nigeria has lived for the entire 2015–2024 window.

## Damodaran 2023 anchors

The dotted reference lines on the overlay chart use the same risk_index convention:

| Country | composite_score | risk_index |
|---|---|---|
| Switzerland | 90 | **10** |
| USA | 80 | **20** |
| South Africa | 56 | 44 |
| Nigeria | 44 | 56 |

The Switzerland and USA lines sit near the bottom of the chart (low risk), and our African peer lines (Nigeria, Ethiopia) sit in the upper half (high risk). The visual gap between the dotted anchors at the bottom and the colored measured lines at the top IS the story of the chart.

## Why this matters for the presentation

When presenting, you can lead with: *"We use the ICRG framework — but we flipped the axis from 'higher = safer' to 'higher = riskier' so the chart reads intuitively. Same data, same methodology as Damodaran 2023; just rotated so a line going up means things are getting worse."*

That single sentence handles the methodology purist in the audience without making the rest of the talk confusing. The chart titles all say `(higher = more risk)` to reinforce.

Full pipeline for the Risk Index, from raw news article to final number:

## Step 1: Each event gets a "score adjustment"

For every event the LLM extracts (e.g., "Buhari election peaceful"), we compute:

```
score_adjustment = severity_weight × direction_sign × tier_weight
```

Where:
| Component | Values |
|---|---|
| **Severity weight** | Low = 0.5 · Medium = 1.0 · High = 1.5 |
| **Direction sign** | Positive = +1 · Negative = −1 · Mixed = −0.5 · Neutral = 0 |
| **Tier weight** | Tier 1 (Reuters/Bloomberg/AP) = 1.0 · Tier 2 (local dailies) = 0.7 · Tier 3 (regional aggregators) = 0.4 |

Example: a high-severity, negative-direction event from Tier-1 source = `1.5 × −1 × 1.0 = −1.5`

## Step 2: Aggregate by quarter and factor

For each quarter and each of the 8 risk factors, sum the score adjustments:

```
total_adjustment[quarter, factor] = sum(score_adjustment for events in that quarter+factor)
```

## Step 3: Build factor scores (start at midpoint, adjust)

Each factor has a max points value and a midpoint:
- Government Stability: max 12, midpoint 6
- Internal Conflict: max 12, midpoint 6
- Corruption: max 6, midpoint 3
- Law & Order: max 6, midpoint 3
- Inflation: max 10, midpoint 5
- GDP Growth: max 10, midpoint 5
- Exchange Rate Stability: max 5, midpoint 2.5
- External Debt / Current Account: max 15, midpoint 7.5

```
factor_score[quarter] = clip(midpoint + total_adjustment, 0, max_points)
```

If no events that quarter for a factor → carries forward previous quarter's score (not the midpoint).

## Step 4: Macro anchoring (override 2 factors with World Bank reality)

For Inflation and GDP Growth, we override the LLM-derived factor with the World Bank rubric anchor:

| WB CPI | Inflation factor anchor |
|---|---|
| < 2% | 10 (best) |
| 2–6% | 7.5 |
| 6–10% | 5 |
| 10–20% | 2.5 |
| > 20% | 0 (worst) |

| WB GDP growth | GDP factor anchor |
|---|---|
| > 6% | 10 |
| 3–6% | 7.5 |
| 0–3% | 5 |
| −3 to 0% | 2.5 |
| < −3% | 0 |

This step ensures the score doesn't drift from reality on macro factors.

## Step 5: Sum factors into pillars (then scale to ICRG ranges)

```
political_score = (Government Stability + Internal Conflict + Corruption + Law & Order)
                  scaled to 0–50  (raw max 36 → 50)
economic_score  = (Inflation + GDP Growth)  scaled to 0–25  (raw 20 → 25)
financial_score = (Exchange Rate + External Debt) scaled to 0–25 (raw 20 → 25)
```

## Step 6: Composite

```
composite_score = political_score + economic_score + financial_score    (range 0–100)
```

This follows the ICRG convention: **higher = SAFER**. Switzerland scores ~90, Nigeria ~44.

## Step 7: Invert for presentation (THIS IS THE RISK INDEX)

```
risk_index = 100 − composite_score
```

That's it. One subtraction. Now **higher = MORE RISK**, which matches audience intuition:
- Switzerland: composite 90 → risk_index 10
- Nigeria worst quarter: composite 37.6 → risk_index 62.4
- Ethiopia worst quarter: composite 31.4 → risk_index 68.6

## End-to-end one-liner

> *"For each news article, the LLM extracts events tagged with one of 8 ICRG risk factors, a direction (better/worse), and a severity. Each event scores severity × direction × source-tier. Quarterly sums adjust factor scores from a midpoint, with World Bank macros anchoring inflation and GDP. Sum factors into 3 pillars, sum pillars into a 0–100 composite, then invert (100 − composite) for presentation so the line goes UP when risk gets worse."*

That sentence is the entire methodology defense.

## How tier weighting works in the composite

For every event the LLM extracts, the score adjustment is:

```
score_adjustment = severity_weight × direction_sign × tier_weight
```

So a **high-severity, negative event from Reuters (Tier 1)** counts as `1.5 × −1 × 1.0 = −1.5`
A **same event from Modern Ghana (Tier 3)** counts as `1.5 × −1 × 0.4 = −0.6`

This means a single Reuters article moves the score **2.5× more** than a regional aggregator covering the same event. The rubric's logic: international wires have stricter editorial standards, so their reporting deserves more weight.
