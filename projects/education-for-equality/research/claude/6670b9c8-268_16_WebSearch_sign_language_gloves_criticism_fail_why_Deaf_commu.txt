## Technical and Linguistic Failures

Sign language translation gloves invented so far misconstrue the nature of ASL (and other sign languages) by focusing on what the hands do. Key parts of the grammar of ASL include "raised or lowered eyebrows, a shift in the orientation of the signer's torso, or a movement of the mouth," and even perfectly functioning gloves would not have access to facial expressions.

Signs don't appear like clearly delineated beads on a string; they bleed into one another in a process that linguists call "coarticulation."

## Audism and Flawed Premises

The criticism extends beyond technical limitations to fundamental issues with the gloves' purpose. This is described as "a manifestation of audist beliefs," representing "the idea that the Deaf person must expend the effort to accommodate to the standards of communication of the hearing person."

The concept of the gloves is to render ASL intelligible to hearing people who don't know how to sign, but this overlooks the communication difficulties and frustrations that Deaf people can already face. Additionally, the deaf person wearing the gloves would sign to a hearing person and the gloves would translate the signs into speech, but the hearing person wouldn't be able to speak their response because the deaf person cannot hear it and they wouldn't be able to sign their response because they clearly don't know a signed language.

## Lack of Deaf Community Involvement

The use of ASL "translation" as a case study for these motion capture gloves without any involvement of the Deaf community is another example of cultural appropriation. Many school and college projects, which are touted as technology which will help deaf people to communicate with the hearing community, are initiated almost exclusively by hearing people.

## Practical Concerns

As a technology, the sign-language glove has more to do with the preoccupations of hearing people than a genuine attempt to understand Deaf people's needs.
