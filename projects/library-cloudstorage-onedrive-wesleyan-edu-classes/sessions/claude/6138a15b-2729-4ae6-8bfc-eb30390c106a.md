# Diamond & Dybvig (1983) — "Bank Runs, Deposit Insurance, and Liquidity"

This is **the** paper. Your whole class is built on it. Your DepositCoin paper extends it. Prof. Izumi's lectures derive everything from it. You need to know it cold.

Here's the whole thing, in beginner language.

---

## The big idea in one paragraph

Why do banks exist? Because people don't know when they'll need their money. A bank pools everyone together and gives them better insurance against that uncertainty than they could get alone. But the very contract that provides this insurance — the demand deposit — has a hidden flaw: it has *two possible outcomes*. One is great (everyone is better off). The other is a bank run (everyone panics, the bank dies, everyone loses). The paper shows that **government deposit insurance** can eliminate the bad outcome at zero cost in the good outcome.

That's the whole paper. The rest is showing it formally.

---

## Section II — The setup (the world in three periods)

### The timing

Three dates: **t = 0**, **t = 1**, **t = 2**. Think of them as "now," "midway," and "later."

### The technology

There's one investment you can make at t = 0. It has two possible payoffs:
- **Cash it in at t = 1**: you get back exactly **1** (just your money, no profit)
- **Wait until t = 2**: you get **R** > 1 (a real return, say R = 1.10 for 10%)

So the investment is **illiquid** — you pay a penalty for getting out early. This is just like Lecture 3's "long-term project pays 2 at t=2 but only 1 if liquidated at t=1."

### The agents

Lots of people, each starts with **1 unit** of endowment at t = 0. At t = 1, each person learns their **type**:
- **Type 1 (impatient)**: only cares about consuming at t = 1
- **Type 2 (patient)**: only cares about consuming at t = 2

A fraction **t** (lowercase t, confusing — call it **π** like in Lecture 11) of people are Type 1. So if π = 0.5, half need money early.

**The critical detail:** *your type is private information.* Nobody else can see whether you're truly impatient or you're lying. This is the whole problem — if types were observable, the bank could just check your ID and pay you accordingly. They aren't.

### What people want

Each person has a utility function `u(c)` where c is what they consume. It's **concave** (diminishing returns) — meaning getting from $1,000 to $2,000 feels better than getting from $10,000 to $11,000. They're risk-averse.

---

## Section II continued — Three possible worlds

### World 1 — Autarky (no bank, just hold the investment yourself)

You put your 1 into the technology. At t = 1, you learn your type:
- If Type 1: liquidate, get **1**
- If Type 2: wait, get **R**

That's it. No risk sharing. Type 1's are stuck with 1; Type 2's get R.

### World 2 — The first-best (what we'd do if types were observable)

This is the dream world. Everyone gets the optimal allocation:
- Type 1 gets **c₁\*** (something bigger than 1 but smaller than R)
- Type 2 gets **c₂\*** (something bigger than 1 but smaller than R)

The math (equation 1 in the paper):
- `c₂* > c₁* > 1` — patient people get more, but impatient people now also get more than just their money back
- Resource constraint: `π·c₁* + (1−π)·c₂*/R = 1`

This is the famous **(c₁\*, c₂\*)** pair you've seen all over Econ 333. **It's strictly better than autarky** because the impatient people get insured against being unlucky.

The catch: you can't actually offer this contract because **types are private**. Without observability, why would the patient people honestly admit they're patient and accept less today? Hmm.

### World 3 — The bank (a clever solution)

The bank offers a **demand deposit contract**: "Deposit your 1 with me. Withdraw anytime at t = 1 and get **r₁** per unit. Otherwise you'll get your share of whatever's left at t = 2."

Set **r₁ = c₁\***. Now magic happens:

- A Type 1 (impatient) shows up at t = 1 and withdraws r₁ = c₁\*. ✓
- A Type 2 (patient) waits — because if everyone behaves correctly, they'll get c₂\* > c₁\* at t = 2. ✓

**The bank just achieved the first-best without observing types.** Each type self-selects into the right action.

This is the bank's whole reason to exist: **liquidity insurance through pooling.**

---

## Section II — But there's a catch: the bank run equilibrium

Here's the problem the paper is famous for.

The contract `r₁ = c₁* > 1` works perfectly **if everyone trusts each other**. But it also has a second possible outcome:

Suppose Type 2 (a patient person) believes everyone *else* will run at t = 1. What should she do?

- If she waits: the bank will run out of assets paying everyone else, and she'll get **zero**.
- If she also runs: she gets c₁\* (or maybe something, depending on her place in line).

So she runs too. **Everyone runs. The bank dies.** Everyone gets a random partial payout.

This is the **bank run equilibrium**. It's "self-fulfilling" — it happens *because* people expect it to happen. There's no fundamental reason; **sunspots** would trigger it (literally a phrase the paper uses, footnote 8). Any random event that shifts expectations can do it.

So the demand deposit contract has **two Nash equilibria**:
1. **Good NE**: only Type 1's withdraw early, everyone gets first-best
2. **Bad NE (run)**: everyone withdraws early, bank liquidates, everyone loses

This is the exact "two equilibria" structure you saw in Lecture 7–8 and that your DepositCoin model still uses.

---

## Section III — Fix #1: Suspension of convertibility

The first proposed solution. The bank announces: **"I will only let the first π fraction of you withdraw at t = 1. After that, the window closes."**

Why this works:
- A Type 2 thinking about running knows: "If I try to run, I might be too late and get nothing at t = 1. But if I wait, I'm *guaranteed* my c₂\* at t = 2 — because the bank can't be drained past the suspension point."
- So Type 2's never run, no matter what they expect others to do.
- Only Type 1's withdraw. The bank survives. First-best is achieved.

**When does this work?** Only when π (the fraction of Type 1's) is **known with certainty**. If you don't know exactly how many impatient people there are, suspending too early hurts genuine impatient people who didn't get served. Suspending too late lets a run drain the bank.

This was actually used historically — banks would close their doors during panics. Friedman & Schwartz (1963) argue suspensions worked before the Fed existed.

---

## Section IV — Why suspension isn't enough (stochastic withdrawals)

Now make π **random**. Sometimes there are many impatient people, sometimes few. The bank doesn't know which.

**Proposition 1 (paper's first main result):** When π is random, **no bank contract alone can achieve the first-best.** The bank is stuck with imperfect outcomes — either it suspends too aggressively (hurts genuine Type 1's) or it stays vulnerable to runs.

This is the impossibility result. The bank needs help from outside.

---

## Section V — Fix #2: Government deposit insurance (the headline result)

Here's where the paper drops the hammer.

**The government** has one thing the bank doesn't: **the power to tax**. It can collect money from people *after* they've withdrawn. The bank can't do that — its sequential service constraint means once it pays you, it can't claw it back.

**The deposit insurance scheme** works like this:
- The bank promises everyone c₁\* at t = 1 if they withdraw
- The government promises: "If the bank can't pay, *we* will, using taxes"
- Specifically, the tax function τ(f) makes after-tax payouts equal to:
  - Type 1 withdrawing: gets c₁\*(t) — full optimal amount
  - Type 2 waiting: gets c₂\*(t) — full optimal amount

**Proposition 2 (paper's second main result):** Demand deposit contracts with government deposit insurance achieve the first-best as a **unique dominant-strategy equilibrium.** 

What does that mean in English? **No matter what anyone thinks anyone else will do, the unique best response is to act according to your true type.** Type 1's withdraw early, Type 2's wait. Always. No run equilibrium exists anymore.

**The truly magical part:** in equilibrium, the insurance is **never used**. The mere *credible promise* to pay eliminates the panic. No tax actually gets collected because no one runs. The insurance is costless in equilibrium.

(This is exactly the slide your professor showed you in Lecture 11: "if `g > g^T`, runs are eliminated AND no DI payments are made — the policy has no direct cost.")

---

## Section VI — Conclusions and three side points

1. **Deposit insurance vs. lender of last resort**: The Fed's discount window can serve a similar role — buying bank assets at fair value during a crisis to prevent liquidation. With a *riskless* technology like this paper's, they're equivalent. With risky assets, DI is more credible because LoLR creates moral hazard ("they'll bail us out, so let's take big risks").

2. **The model generalizes beyond banks**: Any firm with illiquid assets and short-term debt has the same vulnerability — that's why corporations have credit lines from banks, and why bankruptcy law exists (it's a kind of suspension of convertibility for firms).

3. **The model abstracts a lot**: no currency, no risky technology, no aggregate uncertainty in the basic version. Extensions add these.

# Every equation in Diamond-Dybvig (1983), explained

I'll go in paper order. For each equation I'll show: **what it says in symbols → what it means in plain English → a numerical example with class-style numbers → why it matters.**

Throughout, I'll use **π** instead of the paper's lowercase `t` for "fraction of impatient types" (matches Lecture 7 / Izumi's notation). I'll also drop the paper's "ρ" (a utility discount factor) and just assume ρ = 1, like your class does — it doesn't change the structure.

---

## Equation 1: The production technology

**What the paper writes (the table on p. 405):**

```
              t=0       t=1            t=2
Invest:       -1        {0  or  R }
                        {1  or  0 }
```

**What it means:** Put 1 unit in at t = 0. Then **at t = 1 you choose**:
- Cash out early → get **1** back (and nothing at t = 2)
- Wait → get **R** at t = 2 (and nothing at t = 1), where R > 1

**Class-style numerical example:** R = 2.
- Wait until t = 2 → get $2
- Liquidate at t = 1 → get $1 (just your principal, no profit)

**Why it matters:** This is the *only source of fragility* in the entire model. The asset is illiquid — getting out early costs you the return. Every result downstream depends on this single asymmetry. In your paper this becomes the (r, R) pair in the bank's portfolio.

---

## Equation 2: The state-dependent utility function

**What the paper writes:**

```
U(c₁, c₂; θ) = { u(c₁)            if you're Type 1
               { ρ · u(c₁ + c₂)   if you're Type 2
```

with `ρ` somewhere between 1/R and 1, and `u(·)` increasing, concave, with relative risk aversion > 1.

**What it means:** Two types of people:
- **Type 1 (impatient):** only cares about t = 1 consumption. The c₂ doesn't appear in their utility — they get zero pleasure from waiting.
- **Type 2 (patient):** consumes at t = 2. But notice: their utility depends on `c₁ + c₂`. Why both? Because if Type 2 *receives* goods at t = 1, they can store them privately at no cost and still consume at t = 2.

**Concrete example (class log-utility):** `u(c) = ln(c)`. If a Type 1 gets c₁ = 1.5, their utility is ln(1.5) ≈ 0.405. If a Type 2 gets c₁ = 0 and c₂ = 1.8, their utility is ln(0 + 1.8) ≈ 0.588.

**Why it matters:** The fact that utility is **concave** (diminishing marginal utility) is what makes insurance valuable. A risk-averse Type 1 strongly prefers a guaranteed 1.5 to a coin flip between 1 and 2 — even though both have the same expected value. That preference is what banks exploit.

---

## Equations (1a), (1b), (1c): The first-best optimal allocation

This is **the** important set of equations — the (c₁\*, c₂\*) pair you've seen all over Econ 333.

### (1a) — The self-selection corner condition

```
c₁²* = c₂¹* = 0
```

**What it means:** At the optimum, the bank gives **zero** to Type 1 at t = 2 (they don't want it anyway), and **zero** to Type 2 at t = 1 (they're better off waiting). This eliminates the redundant variables; we only need to figure out c₁\* (what Type 1 gets) and c₂\* (what Type 2 gets).

### (1b) — The marginal-utility condition (Euler equation)

```
u'(c₁*) = ρ · R · u'(c₂*)
```

**What it means:** "At the optimum, you can't make society better off by shifting one more unit of consumption from Type 1 to Type 2 (or vice versa)." The marginal utility of one more dollar to Type 1, weighted by 1, must equal the marginal utility to Type 2, weighted by R (because Type 2's dollar grew at rate R) and by ρ.

**Class log-utility example:** With `u(c) = ln(c)`, `u'(c) = 1/c`. Plug in, with ρ = 1:

```
1/c₁* = R · (1/c₂*)
→ c₂* = R · c₁*
```

So with R = 2: `c₂* = 2 · c₁*`. Patient people get exactly **twice** what impatient people get.

### (1c) — The resource constraint

```
π · c₁* + (1 − π) · c₂*/R = 1
```

**What it means:** The bank's total resources balance. Each person deposited 1 at t = 0, so total resources = 1 (per person on average). Of those resources:
- A fraction π of people are Type 1, each gets c₁\* at t = 1. Cost per person: π · c₁\*
- A fraction (1 − π) are Type 2, each gets c₂\* at t = 2. But c₂\* is paid at t = 2, so its "present value at t = 0" is c₂\*/R. Cost per person: (1 − π) · c₂\*/R
- Total cost must equal 1

**Numerical example with R = 2, π = 0.5, log utility:**

From (1b): `c₂* = 2 · c₁*`
Sub into (1c): `0.5 · c₁* + 0.5 · (2c₁*)/2 = 1` → `0.5·c₁* + 0.5·c₁* = 1` → `c₁* = 1` and `c₂* = 2`.

Hmm — that's just autarky! In log utility with these specific numbers, the bank doesn't actually help. That's because log utility has relative risk aversion exactly = 1, but the paper assumes > 1 strictly.

**Better example with relative risk aversion = 2** (utility `u(c) = −1/c`): you'd get something like c₁\* ≈ 1.2 and c₂\* ≈ 1.6, with R = 2. **Both types better off than autarky** (where Type 1 got 1 and Type 2 got 2). That's the gain from pooling.

**Why these three equations matter:** They define the **target outcome**. Everything in the rest of the paper is about whether the bank, with various tools, can hit this target. Lectures 9–11 in your class are all about deriving these or modifying them with policy variables.

---

## Footnote 3: The proof that c₁\* > 1 and c₂\* < R

This is the formal proof of the headline result of Section II. The paper's chain is:

```
ρRu'(R) < Ru'(R)
       = 1·u'(1) + ∫[γ=1 to R] (∂/∂γ)[γu'(γ)] dγ
       = u'(1) + ∫[γ=1 to R] [u'(γ) + γu''(γ)] dγ
       < u'(1)
```

**What's happening:** The paper is showing that the marginal utility you'd get from the autarky outcome (c₂ = R for Type 2) is **smaller** than the marginal utility from c₁ = 1 (autarky for Type 1). That's the formal way of saying "Type 1 is hungrier for an extra dollar than Type 2 is."

**The intuition without the integral:** Type 1 in autarky gets only 1. Type 2 in autarky gets R > 1 — they're richer. Because of diminishing marginal utility, the *poor* Type 1 values an extra dollar more than the *rich* Type 2 does. So at the optimum, you'd shift consumption from Type 2 to Type 1 → c₁\* > 1 and c₂\* < R.

**Why it matters:** This is the proof that **the bank's optimal allocation strictly improves on autarky.** It's the foundation for why banks exist at all in this framework.

## Equation (2): Period 1 payoff under demand deposit

**What the paper writes:**

```
V₁(fⱼ, r₁) = { r₁    if  fⱼ < 1/r₁
             { 0     if  fⱼ ≥ 1/r₁
```

**What it means:** If you're person j and you show up at t = 1 to withdraw, you get one of two things:
- **r₁** (the promised amount, like $1.20) — if you arrive before the bank runs out
- **0** — if you arrive after the bank is empty

The bank starts with 1 per depositor. If it pays r₁ per withdrawal, it can service at most **1/r₁** of depositors before going bust. `fⱼ` is your position in line (as a fraction of total depositors). So if `fⱼ < 1/r₁`, you're early enough; otherwise you're left holding nothing.

**Example:** r₁ = 1.20, so 1/r₁ ≈ 0.833. If 100% of depositors run, the bank can only pay the first 83% of them. The last 17% get $0. **Sequential service** means once you've paid the first person their $1.20, you can't claw it back.

**Why it matters:** This is the formula that makes a run *possible*. The fact that early withdrawers get full r₁ and late ones get 0 creates a panic incentive — if you think others will run, you'd rather be early than late.

## Equation (3): Period 2 payoff under demand deposit

**What the paper writes:**

```
V₂(f, r₁) = max { R(1 − r₁·f) / (1 − f) ,  0 }
```

**What it means:** If you wait until t = 2, you get a pro-rata share of what's left. Let's parse it:
- `f` = total fraction who withdrew at t = 1
- `r₁·f` = total dollars paid out at t = 1
- `1 − r₁·f` = what's left of the bank's deposits (per capita)
- That remainder stays in the productive technology, so it grows to `R·(1 − r₁·f)` by t = 2
- `1 − f` = fraction of depositors still waiting
- So each waiter gets `R·(1 − r₁·f) / (1 − f)`
- The `max{·, 0}` just handles the case where the bank already went bust

**Example with R = 2, r₁ = 1.20:**
- If only Type 1's withdraw (f = π = 0.5): waiters get `2·(1 − 1.20·0.5) / (1 − 0.5)` = `2·0.40 / 0.5` = **1.60** each ✓ (matches optimal c₂\*)
- If everyone runs (f = 1): formula goes to `2·(1 − 1.20)/0 = max{negative/0, 0} = 0`. Waiters get nothing.

**Why it matters:** This formula shows the **collapse mechanism**. As more people run (f rises), each remaining waiter gets *less*. At some critical mass, V₂ drops below r₁ — and that's when patient people start running too. That's the bad NE.

---

## Footnote 6: Verifying the good equilibrium

The paper checks: if `r₁ = c₁*` and only Type 1's withdraw, do we actually get the first-best?

**Substitute f = π and r₁ = c₁\* into (2) and (3):**
- V₁ = c₁\* ✓ (Type 1 gets their optimal amount)
- V₂ = R(1 − π·c₁\*)/(1 − π) — and from (1c), this equals c₂\* ✓ (Type 2 gets their optimal amount)

**Why it matters:** This confirms that the demand deposit contract with `r₁ = c₁\*` **decentralizes the first-best** as one of its equilibria. Patient people, seeing they'll get c₂\* > c₁\*, voluntarily wait. The good NE exists. (Of course, so does the bad NE — that's the whole problem.)

## Section III: Suspension of convertibility — the redefined payoffs

```
V₁(fⱼ, r₁) = { r₁    if  fⱼ ≤ f̂        ← f̂ is the "suspension point"
             { 0     if  fⱼ > f̂

V₂(f, r₁) = max { (1 − f·r₁)·R / (1 − f) ,  (1 − f̂·r₁)·R / (1 − f̂) }
```

**What it means:** The bank announces in advance: *"I will pay r₁ to the first f̂ fraction of withdrawers, then I close the window."*

- V₁: same as before, but truncated at f̂. After f̂, no more withdrawals allowed at t = 1.
- V₂: at t = 2, you get whichever is larger — the standard pro-rata share, or the floor guarantee assuming exactly f̂ people withdrew.

### The condition on f̂

```
f̂ ∈ [π, (R − r₁) / (r₁(R − 1))]
```

**What this range means:**
- **Lower bound π**: f̂ has to be at least π, otherwise genuine Type 1's get cut off (bad ex-post outcome)
- **Upper bound** `(R − r₁)/(r₁(R − 1))`: f̂ has to be small enough that V₂ > V₁ even if f̂ people actually withdraw. This ensures Type 2's *strictly prefer* to wait, regardless of expectations.

**Example with R = 2, r₁ = 1.20, π = 0.5:**
- Upper bound = (2 − 1.20)/(1.20·1) = 0.80/1.20 ≈ 0.667
- So f̂ can be anywhere in [0.5, 0.667]. The bank picks f̂ = 0.5 (the lower bound), exactly matching the number of Type 1's.

**Why it matters:** Suspension creates a **dominant strategy** for Type 2: wait. No matter what they think others will do, V₂ > V₁ always. The bad NE is eliminated when π is known. The paper's first fix.

## Section IV: Proposition 1 — the impossibility when π is random

No equation, but a key result: **when π is random, no bank contract can achieve the first-best.** The proof is by contradiction (paper p. 412), showing that any V₁ depending on `fⱼ` either varies across Type 1's (violating optimality) or stays constant (violating the budget constraint when π fluctuates).

**Why it matters:** This motivates Section V. The bank alone isn't enough. You need the government.

## Section V: The tax function for deposit insurance

**What the paper writes:**

```
τ(f) = { 1 − c₁*(f)/r₁     if  f ≤ t̄
       { 1 − 1/r₁           if  f > t̄
```

where t̄ is the largest possible value of π.

**What it means:** A proportional tax on whoever shows up to withdraw at t = 1. The bank still promises r₁, but the government claws back some fraction τ via taxes, so the *net* amount each withdrawer keeps is what they should get under the first-best.

**Decoding the first case (f ≤ t̄):**
- The bank pays r₁ to each withdrawer
- The optimal Type 1 consumption (given realized π = f) is `c₁*(f)`
- So the tax has to bring r₁ down to c₁*(f): tax rate = 1 − c₁*(f)/r₁
- The collected tax stays in the bank's investment, boosting payouts at t = 2

**Why it matters:** The government can tax *after* someone has already withdrawn (the bank can't — sequential service). That asymmetry is the key power that lets DI achieve what the bank alone can't.

- And — crucially — in equilibrium, the tax is never actually invoked at distortionary levels, because there's no run

# ρ (rho) — the time-discount factor

## What it is

ρ is **Type 2's patience factor** — how much they discount future consumption.

The utility function in the paper says:
```
Type 1's utility: u(c₁)              ← no ρ, they consume right now
Type 2's utility: ρ · u(c₁ + c₂)     ← ρ multiplies in
```

The ρ shows up only for Type 2 because *they're the ones doing the waiting.* It captures the idea: "even patient people slightly prefer a dollar today over a dollar tomorrow."

**Examples:**
- ρ = 1.0 → no impatience at all. A dollar at t = 2 is worth exactly the same as a dollar at t = 1.
- ρ = 0.9 → mild impatience. A dollar at t = 2 is worth $0.90 of t = 1 dollars to a Type 2.
- ρ = 0.6 → strong impatience.

## The paper's constraint: 1/R < ρ ≤ 1

The paper requires `ρ` to live in a specific range:

**Upper bound (ρ ≤ 1):** Type 2 isn't *more* patient about future consumption than present — that wouldn't make sense (why would you prefer waiting *just to wait*?).

**Lower bound (ρ > 1/R):** This is the **economically interesting** condition. It says ρ · R > 1, meaning **even after discounting, waiting still beats not waiting** for a Type 2. If ρ were too small, Type 2's would rather just take c₁ today, and the whole "patient vs impatient" distinction collapses — everyone is effectively impatient and the bank can't offer insurance.

Three reasons:

**1. Your class almost certainly does the same.** Lecture 7 / 8 / 11 in Econ 333 strip ρ out to keep the algebra clean. The "patient vs impatient" distinction in your lectures comes purely from *when* each type consumes — not from any time preference. Check Izumi's slides: I'd bet money there's no ρ anywhere.

**2. The structure is identical either way.** Every result in the paper — c₁\* > 1, c₂\* < R, the run NE, the DI fix — goes through exactly the same with ρ = 1 or ρ = 0.9. ρ just shifts the *numerical values* of c₁\* and c₂\*, not the *shape* of the model.

**3. It made my equation (1b) cleaner.** With ρ = 1 the marginal-utility condition becomes:
```

```
instead of:
```
u'(c₁*) = ρ · R · u'(c₂*)    ← the paper's version
```

The "ρR" combined term is sometimes called the **effective discount factor for waiting**. When ρ = 1, it's just R.

- You wanted to do welfare comparisons across different discount rates
- You were modeling really long horizons where time preference is plausibly big
- You were extending DD with stochastic ρ (some recent papers do this)

**For Monday: ignore ρ. Treat the marginal-utility condition as `u'(c₁*) = R · u'(c₂*)` and you're 100% on the same page as Lecture 11.**

# The full DepositCoin model — solved end to end

I'll do this in nine steps, building from standard DD all the way to your contribution. Every formula explained, every substitution shown. Notation matches Lecture 11.

---

## Step 1: The environment

**Three dates:** t = 0, 1, 2.

**Mass 1 of domestic depositors:**
- Each starts with 1 unit at t = 0
- At t = 1, learns their type:
  - **Impatient (Type 1):** fraction π, consumes at t = 1 only
  - **Patient (Type 2):** fraction (1 − π), consumes at t = 2 only
- Type is private information
- Utility: `u(c)` is increasing, concave (risk-averse)

**Two technologies (from Lecture 7):**
- **Storage:** 1 unit in → 1 unit out, at any date
- **Investment:** 1 at t = 0 → **r** at t = 1 if liquidated early (r < 1, so it's bad to liquidate), or **R** at t = 2 if held to maturity (R > 1)

**Bank's portfolio (from Lecture 7-8):** holds fraction `x*` in investment and `(1 − x*)` in storage.

**Foreign retail users (your contribution):** mass M, each contributes a small deposit. Total foreign capital pooled: **K = M × deposit_size**.

---

## Step 2: Solve for the first-best (c₁\*, c₂\*)

The bank picks (c₁\*, c₂\*) to maximize ex-ante expected utility:

```
maximize   π · u(c₁) + (1 − π) · u(c₂)
```

subject to the resource constraint:

```
π · c₁ + (1 − π) · c₂ / R = 1            ... (*)
```

Each impatient depositor gets c₁ at t = 1 (cost: π · c₁). Each patient depositor gets c₂ at t = 2 (present value at t = 0: c₂/R, so cost per patient is (1−π)·c₂/R). Total ≤ 1 (the endowment).

**The first-order conditions (Lagrangian):**

```
u'(c₁*) = R · u'(c₂*)                    ... (Euler)
```

Patient gets more because waiting earns return R.

**Worked example with log utility u(c) = ln(c), R = 2, π = 0.5:**

From Euler: `1/c₁* = 2 · (1/c₂*)` → **c₂\* = 2 · c₁\***

Substitute into (\*): `0.5 · c₁* + 0.5 · (2c₁*)/2 = 1` → `c₁* = 1`, `c₂* = 2`.

(With log utility you get exactly autarky — pick CRRA = 2 utility instead and you'd get c₁\* ≈ 1.17, c₂\* ≈ 1.66, both better than autarky. The class probably uses CRRA > 1.)

---

## Step 3: The bank's portfolio split x\*

Bank wants to hold *just enough* storage to pay the impatient at t = 1 without liquidating investment (because liquidating is wasteful — you get r < 1 instead of R).

**At t = 1**, demand from impatient: `π · c₁*`. Supply from storage: `1 − x*`. Setting them equal:

```
π · c₁* = 1 − x*
→ x* = 1 − π · c₁*                       ... (storage clears t=1)
```

**At t = 2**, investment matures to `R · x*`. Demand from patient: `(1 − π) · c₂*`. Setting them equal:

```
(1 − π) · c₂* = R · x*
→ x* = (1 − π) · c₂* / R                 ... (investment clears t=2)
```

(Both expressions for x\* are consistent — that's just the budget constraint again.)

**Example with R = 2, π = 0.5, c₁\* = 1.17, c₂\* = 1.66:**

x\* = 1 − 0.5 × 1.17 = **0.415** (about 42% of deposits in long-term investment, 58% in storage).

---

## Step 4: The two equilibria

The bank offers each depositor: "withdraw at t = 1 and get c₁\*, or wait and get c₂\*."

**Good NE:** only impatient withdraw. Patient wait. Everyone gets first-best. ✓

**Bad NE (the run):** suppose a fraction `e` of patient types also withdraw early ("e" = "excess" early withdrawers). Bank has to liquidate investment to pay them, because storage only covers π · c₁\*.

Let `ℓ` = fraction of investment liquidated. The bank's t = 1 budget must balance:

```
(π + e) · c₁* = (1 − x*) + ℓ · r · x* + g
```

Left side: total t = 1 withdrawal demand.
Right side: storage (1 − x*) + liquidation proceeds (ℓ · r · x\*) + DI fund (g).

Solve for ℓ:

```
ℓ = [(π + e) · c₁* − (1 − x*) − g] / (r · x*)
```

At t = 2, remaining investment is `(1 − ℓ) · x*`, which grows to `R · (1 − ℓ) · x*`. Number of patient still waiting: `(1 − π − e)`. So:

```
c₂(e) = R · (1 − ℓ) · x* / (1 − π − e)
```

After substituting ℓ and simplifying (using x\* = (1 − π) · c₂\* / R and 1 − x\* = π · c₁\*):

```
c₂(e) = [(1 − π) · c₂* − (R/r) · e · c₁* + (R/r) · g] / (1 − π − e)
```

This is the **central equation**. It says: as more patient types run (e ↑), the payoff to the remaining waiters drops — because liquidation destroys value (R/r > 1 means each liquidated dollar of investment costs the bank R/r dollars at t = 2).

---

## Step 5: The run-elimination threshold g^T

**No-run condition:** even if a patient type believes ALL other patients are running (e = 1 − π), they should still prefer waiting. So we need:

```
c₂(e) ≥ c₁*    for all  e ∈ [0, 1−π]
```

This is a constraint on g. The smallest g that satisfies it is **g^T**. Setting `c₂(e) = c₁*` and solving for g (with the worst-case algebra spelled out in Lecture 11):

```
g^T = (1 − π) · (c₁* − (r/R) · c₂*)
```

**This is THE formula from Lecture 11.** Let's plug numbers:

With R = 2, r = 1, π = 0.5, c₁\* = 1.17, c₂\* = 1.66:

```
g^T = 0.5 · (1.17 − (1/2) · 1.66)
    = 0.5 · (1.17 − 0.83)
    = 0.5 · 0.34
    = 0.17
```

So the bank needs a DI fund of **17 cents per dollar of deposits** to kill the run. In US calibration (with class numbers using log-ish utility), this is ~8% of deposits ≈ **$1.44 trillion**.

The actual FDIC fund (g_dom): **$137 billion** ≈ 0.7% of deposits. **The gap is enormous.**

Government balance sheet at t = 2:

- **Debt obligations:** D₀ (pre-existing debt) + g (DI bonds it had to issue)


**The diabolic loop in two channels (already in your slide deck):**

# What "sovereign solvency" actually means in practice

**Solvency = can the government pay its debts when they come due.**

In practice, a sovereign solvency crisis looks like this, in roughly the order it unfolds:

## 1. Bond yields spike

Investors (foreign and domestic) start demanding higher interest rates to lend to the government, because they're not sure they'll get paid back. A government that used to borrow at 3% suddenly has to borrow at 8%, 12%, 20%.

**Real example:** Greek 10-year yields hit ~35% in 2012 at the peak of the eurozone crisis. Argentina's hit 100%+ during multiple crises.

## 2. Debt rollover problems

Governments don't usually pay off their debt — they **roll it over** (issue new bonds to pay off old ones). When yields spike, rolling over becomes punishingly expensive. Each new bond costs much more than the old one.

If yields go high enough, no one will lend at any price — that's a **sudden stop**.

## 3. Forced austerity

To convince markets they'll be paid back, the government slashes spending: pension cuts, public sector layoffs, frozen wages, healthcare cuts. This is what "IMF conditionality" usually means.

**Real example:** Greece cut pensions ~40% between 2010 and 2015. Public sector wages fell ~25%.

## 4. Currency collapse (for non-USD countries)

Investors flee the currency. The exchange rate crashes. Imports get expensive overnight — fuel, food, medicine.

**Real example:** Argentine peso lost ~87% of its dollar value 2023–2025. Turkish lira ~75% over 2018–2024.

## 5. Bank failures cascade

Banks hold lots of government bonds. When bond prices crash (yields up = prices down), banks' balance sheets crater. Some go insolvent. Depositors panic and run. **This is the diabolic loop closing the circle** — banking crisis caused the sovereign crisis, now the sovereign crisis is causing more banking crises.

## 6. Outright default

The government literally stops paying bondholders, or "restructures" the debt (forcing creditors to take 30–60% haircuts).

**Real examples:**
- Argentina defaulted in 2001 (then again 2014, 2020)
- Greece restructured in 2012 — bondholders took ~53% haircut
- Russia defaulted in 1998
- Lebanon defaulted in 2020

## 7. Real-economy damage (the human cost)

This is what your professor cares about — the welfare loss in the model becomes real:

- **Unemployment** spikes (Greece hit 27% in 2013, youth unemployment 60%)
- **GDP contracts** (Argentina 2001–2002: GDP fell ~28% over two years)
- **Inflation** explodes (Argentina hit 211% in 2023, Venezuela hit hyperinflation)
- **Bank deposits frozen** (Argentina's "corralito" in 2001 — depositors couldn't withdraw their own money for a year)
- **Brain drain, capital flight, political instability**

**(a) Who can issue:** Only "permitted payment stablecoin issuers" — must be a regulated bank subsidiary, a state-chartered trust, or a federally-chartered nonbank approved by the OCC. **No more unregulated stablecoin issuance.** Tether/Circle have to formally apply for permitted status.

**(b) What backs them:** 1:1 reserves in cash, short-term Treasuries (≤93 days), or overnight repos. **No commercial paper, no corporate bonds, no crypto.** This was a direct response to the 2022 Terra/Luna collapse and Tether's historical commercial-paper exposure.

**(c) THE YIELD BAN — Section 4(a)(11):** This is the section you reference repeatedly in your paper. It reads:

> *"No permitted payment stablecoin issuer ... shall pay the holder of any payment stablecoin any form of interest or yield (whether in cash, tokens, or other consideration) solely in connection with the holding, use, or retention of such payment stablecoin."*

In English: **if you hold a stablecoin, you cannot earn interest on it.** Even though the issuer is sitting on T-bill reserves earning 4–5%, none of that yield can be passed through to the holder.

### Why the yield ban exists

Two arguments from the legislative record:
1. **Bank competition concern** — if stablecoins paid yield, they'd compete directly with bank deposits and drain bank funding. Big banks lobbied hard for this.
2. **Securities-law concern** — anything paying yield risks being classified as a security (Howey test). The yield ban keeps stablecoins firmly in the "payment instrument" lane.

### What this means for your paper

The yield ban is **your legal keyhole**. It bans yield "solely in connection with holding" — but it does *not* ban returns paid as compensation for **risk-bearing**. The DepositCoin claim is structured exactly to thread this needle: foreign users absorb first-loss in a US bank run (real risk), and in exchange receive compensation that is legally a risk premium, not interest on holding.

# Pre-GENIUS: how stablecoins existed in a legal gray zone

Stablecoins existed and grew to a **$130B+ market by mid-2024** without any federal framework. The short answer: **they weren't illegal, but they weren't really legal either** — they operated through a patchwork of state licenses, regulatory non-action, and aggressive interpretation of existing statutes. Here's the actual mechanism.

---

## 1. State money transmitter licenses — the main legal basis

Before GENIUS, the dominant legal theory was: **a stablecoin issuer is a money transmitter**, like Western Union or PayPal.

**How this worked:**
- Money transmission is regulated **state by state** in the US (49 states + DC require licenses)
- Issuers like Circle and Paxos got money transmitter licenses (MTLs) in most states
- Each state has its own rules on reserves, audits, and net worth — patchwork compliance
- New York's BitLicense (2015) was the strictest framework and became the de facto national standard

**Circle (USDC):** got MTLs across ~46 states + a NY BitLicense
**Paxos (BUSD, USDP):** state-chartered trust company under NYDFS
**Tether (USDT):** **never got NY licensure** — fled NY in 2017 after the Attorney General investigation, operated from offshore (BVI, then El Salvador)

This is why **USDT was always legally sketchier than USDC**: USDT operated largely outside US regulatory perimeter, USDC operated mostly inside it.

---

## 2. The NY BitLicense + NY Trust Charter — the gold standard pre-GENIUS

**NYDFS (New York Department of Financial Services)** became the strictest pre-GENIUS regulator. Two paths under NY law:

**(a) BitLicense (2015)** — for crypto businesses generally
**(b) NY Limited Purpose Trust Charter** — bank-adjacent status under NY Banking Law

Paxos and (later) Gemini got the **trust charter**, which gave them quasi-bank treatment. Under this:
- Reserves had to be 1:1 in cash and Treasuries (NYDFS rule, not federal law)
- Monthly attestations required
- NYDFS could examine the issuer like a bank
- Customer assets were legally segregated from issuer's

This is why pre-GENIUS, you'd hear people say "USDC is regulated" or "Gemini Dollar is regulated" — they meant *state-regulated*, mostly by NY. No federal framework existed.

- Required FDIC-supervised banks to notify the FDIC before engaging with crypto firms
- Didn't ban it, but created friction
- This is why most US banks wouldn't touch stablecoin issuers

**Tether (USDT)** is the world's largest stablecoin: a privately-issued digital token pegged 1:1 to the US dollar. As of 2025–2026, **its circulating supply is over $140 billion**, making it the most widely used dollar-equivalent token in the world by a wide margin.

---

## The basics

- **Token symbol:** USDT
- **Issuer:** Tether Holdings Limited
- **Founded:** 2014 (originally as "Realcoin," renamed Tether)
- **Domicile:** British Virgin Islands, with operations more recently in El Salvador
- **Peg:** 1 USDT is meant to always be redeemable for $1 USD
- **Backing (claimed):** cash, short-term US Treasuries, and other reserve assets held by Tether Holdings

---

## How it works in one paragraph

A user sends $1 of fiat to Tether. Tether mints 1 USDT and gives it to them. The user can then send that USDT to anyone, anywhere, on multiple blockchains (Ethereum, Tron, etc.) in seconds for fractions of a cent. To redeem, the user sends USDT back to Tether and gets $1 back. The promise is that there's always $1 of real assets backing every 1 USDT in circulation. **Whether that promise is actually kept has been a recurring controversy.**

---

## Why it dominates emerging markets

USDT is the **default dollar substitute** in most of the developing world:

- **Turkey:** USDT/TRY pair on Binance hit ~$22B annual volume in 2024 — roughly half of all Bitcoin trading on local exchanges
- **Nigeria, Argentina, Vietnam, Indonesia:** USDT dominates retail crypto markets, often used as a savings vehicle, not for speculation
- **Reason:** USDT trades on more exchanges, in more currency pairs, with deeper liquidity than any competitor — and works on Tron blockchain where transaction fees are basically zero, ideal for poor users sending small amounts

If you go to a P2P crypto market in Lagos or Buenos Aires, what people are buying is almost always USDT, not USDC.

---

## The controversy — why it matters for your paper

Three things make Tether structurally suspect compared to USDC:

**1. No full audit, ever.** Tether has only ever produced **"attestations"** (a partial accounting snapshot) — never a full audit by a Big Four firm. For 11+ years. This is unique among major financial entities of its size.

**2. The 2021 CFTC settlement.** Tether paid $41M to settle CFTC charges that it had **lied about being 1:1 backed**. The CFTC found Tether's reserves were "insufficient" to back the issued USDT for the majority of the sample period — specifically, fully backed only **27.6% of the time** between 2016–2018.

**3. The 2021 NY Attorney General settlement.** Tether paid $18.5M and was **banned from operating in New York State**. The NY AG specifically found Tether had misrepresented its reserves.

**4. Historical reserve composition was risky.** Before 2022, Tether's reserves included substantial commercial paper (Chinese commercial paper specifically, by some accounts), secured loans to affiliated entities, and even Bitcoin. Only after intense pressure post-Terra/Luna collapse did Tether shift to majority Treasuries.

---

## How GENIUS affects Tether

This is where it gets interesting for your paper. GENIUS:
- Requires permitted issuer status — Tether's BVI/El Salvador structure doesn't qualify directly
- Bans the commercial paper / risky reserve composition Tether historically used
- Requires monthly attestations and (eventually) annual audits
- Caps reserve duration at 93-day Treasuries

**Tether's response (2025–2026):** Created a US-domiciled subsidiary to issue a US-compliant version while continuing to issue the global version offshore. The "two Tethers" structure — onshore for US users, offshore for everyone else.

**Most USDT users in emerging markets use the offshore version**, which technically isn't authorized for US distribution but circulates freely on global exchanges. **GENIUS doesn't have extraterritorial reach.**

---

Tether is the **empirical proof** of two things your paper needs:

**1. The demand is enormous.** $140B+ in circulation, with most of it held by EM retail users. People in Nigeria, Argentina, Turkey will hold a sketchy offshore-issued token with no audit and a history of fraud settlements — because the alternative (holding naira/peso/lira) is worse.
