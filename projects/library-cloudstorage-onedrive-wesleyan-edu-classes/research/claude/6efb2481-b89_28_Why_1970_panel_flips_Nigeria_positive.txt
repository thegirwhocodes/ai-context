## 1. TL;DR

The 1970–1999 portion of the Nigerian record is dominated by three episodes — the post-Biafran-war reconstruction, the 1973–81 oil boom that funded the 1976 Universal Primary Education (UPE) scheme, and the 1981–95 oil-bust / SAP / military-rule collapse — that drive primary GER and GDP per capita strongly *together*, in the same direction, for entirely non-causal reasons. Because OOSC = 100 − GER, when GER and GDP move together, OOSC and GDP move *oppositely*; when this within-Nigeria comovement is mechanically much stronger than the SSA-wide comovement, the **interaction term** picks it up as a positive Nigeria-specific deviation, flipping β₁ + β₂. Pre-1990 WDI Nigerian enrollment numbers are also of low and uneven quality, with administrative GER pegged off uncertain UN population denominators that were heavily revised in the 1990s. Restricting the sample to 2000+ is therefore the empirically and historically defensible choice, and the wrong-sign pre-2000 result is itself evidence for the resource-curse human-capital channel of Gylfason (2001).

## 4. Mechanism #1 — Why Pre-2000 OOSC and GDP Moved Together for Non-Causal Reasons

This is the heart of the puzzle. Three episodes inside the 1970–1999 window each produce a *positive within-Nigeria correlation between GER and GDP* (equivalently, a *negative within-Nigeria correlation between OOSC and GDP* on the *raw* data — but, crucially, one that is **stronger than the SSA average**, which is what the interaction picks up). Because the interaction fits the deviation of Nigeria's slope from the pooled SSA slope, anything that makes Nigerian (GER, GDP) co-movement *more* extreme than the SSA average shifts β₂ upward; combined with β₁'s SSA-average negative coefficient on OOSC, this mechanically pushes β₁ + β₂ toward (and past) zero.

### 4a. The 1973–81 oil boom funded UPE, which was implemented during peak GDP

The 1976 UPE scheme was *announced* in 1974 by General Gowon and *implemented in September 1976* under General Murtala / Obasanjo. It was financed *directly* out of oil revenue: Nigerian foreign exchange earnings from crude exports rose from ₦612M in 1972 to ₦5.06B in 1974 (>8×), and federal oil revenue financed a near-instantaneous capital project to build primary schools and hire teachers (Bevan, Collier, and Gunning 1999; Csapo 1981, *Comparative Education* 17(3): 311–319).

The scale of the resulting enrollment shock was enormous. In April 1976, Niger State had 244 primary schools serving 15,796 pupils; by September 1976 the state needed 931 schools for an additional 63,384 pupils — a 400% growth rate (Aluede 2006, *J. Soc. Sci.*). State-level first-year primary enrollment rose 557% in Niger, 491% in Kano, 442% in Kaduna, and 263% in Benue between the 1975/76 and 1976/77 school years (Csapo 1981; Stock 1985, *Tijdschrift voor Economische en Sociale Geografie*, "The Rise and Fall of Universal Primary Education in Peripheral Northern Nigeria"). National primary GER went from 49.0% (1976) to 94.2% (1980) to **110.6% (1982)** in the WDI series — overshoot reflects late-entering and over-age students entering primary school.

This means the **single largest within-Nigeria GER expansion (≈ +60 pp) coincided exactly with the single largest oil-boom GDP expansion (≈ +30% in per-capita terms)**. The two series rose together not because human capital caused growth, but because oil revenue funded both. Within Nigeria 1976–1981, the partial correlation of GER and GDP per capita is mechanically positive — substantially more positive than for the SSA average over the same window (most SSA countries had no oil rents to fund a UPE-style expansion). Adeolu and Fadayomi (2015, "The Impact of 1976 Universal Primary Education Program on Schooling Attainment in Nigeria") use UPE as a quasi-natural experiment and find an *attainment* effect of 0.8–1.0 years — but the GDP-OOSC *contemporaneous* comovement they document is a fiscal-mechanical correlation, not a production-function relationship.

### 4b. The 1981–86 oil bust + 1986 SAP collapsed both OOSC and GDP together

Brent prices collapsed in 1981–86 and Nigeria's oil-bust GDP per capita fell from $2,080 (1980) to $1,610 (1986) — a 23% per-capita contraction. Babangida signed Nigeria's SAP with the IMF/World Bank in 1986. The SAP imposed naira devaluation (the official rate fell from ₦0.89/$ to >₦7/$), subsidy removal, and federal-budget compression. Inflation rose from 5.4% (1986) to 40.9% (1989) to 72.8% (1995); external debt rose from $19B (1985) to $33B (1993).

For education: between 1984 and 1988, education's share of the Nigerian federal budget fell ≈ 8 percentage points; public spending per primary student fell 32.96% (Geo-Jaja and Mangum, "Education under Structural Adjustment," *McGill Journal of Education*; Hinchliffe 1989, "The impact of economic recession on UPE in Nigeria," *International Journal of Educational Development*). Primary GER fell from 110.3 (1983) to 89.6 (1986) to 81.8 (1988) — a 28-point collapse in five years. Hinchliffe documents that the decline was sharpest for girls and in the rural North.

So between 1983 and 1986, *both GER and GDP per capita fell 20–25%*. That is again a positive within-Nigeria comovement (GER and GDP both falling) — equivalently OOSC rising as GDP falls — but the causal arrow runs from oil prices to *both* education spending *and* GDP, with no direct human-capital-to-GDP channel. Iyoha and Oriakhi (2002, "Explaining African Economic Growth Performance: The Case of Nigeria") characterize the entire 1981–95 macro path as exchange-rate-and-debt-driven, with sectoral growth-accounting decompositions showing that human capital plays essentially no role in the variance of Nigerian GDP growth in this period.

### 4c. The 1985–98 military-rule era amplified the comovement

Buhari (1984–85), Babangida (1985–93), Interim Government (1993), Abacha (1993–98), Abubakar (1998–99). The Abacha regime in particular is associated with capital flight (~$5B/year), education-sector neglect (federal education spending fell to ≈ 0.7% of GDP by 1998), and the second documented GER collapse (the 76.8% trough in 1996). At the same time the 1995 PPP GDP per capita ($1,851) was still 17% below its 1977 peak. The country was simultaneously in an education collapse and an income stagnation, but both were caused by the same regime — political instability and fiscal mismanagement of oil rents — not by an education-to-growth channel.

### 4d. Why this flips the *Nigeria-specific* β

In the within-country variance over 1970–99, Nigeria has:
- One enormous oil-funded GER+GDP expansion (1976–81)
- One enormous oil-bust GER+GDP collapse (1981–86)
- A second, smaller, military-era GER+GDP stagnation (1990–96)

Most other SSA countries do **not** have these synchronized shocks. They have steady (slow, demographics-driven) GER expansion, decoupled from oil-price-driven GDP volatility. So Nigeria's *within-country* (GER, GDP) covariance — equivalently, its *within-country* (OOSC, GDP) covariance — is much more strongly negative (OOSC ↓ as GDP ↑) than the pooled SSA average.

Now look at what your interaction does. β₁ is the SSA-pooled within-country slope; β₁ + β₂ is the Nigeria-specific within-country slope. If Nigeria's slope is *more strongly negative* than the SSA average, β₂ should be *more negative* — i.e. β₁ + β₂ more negative. That is what happens 2000–2024.

But in 1970–99 the comovement is so non-linear and so dominated by the UPE expansion (where GER overshoots 100% — i.e., OOSC mechanically goes negative — while GDP is at its pre-2003 peak) that the OLS line through Nigerian (OOSC, GDP) points has a *small slope or even slightly positive slope* once you net out fixed effects. UPE-era points sit at (negative OOSC, high GDP); 1990s points sit at (high OOSC, low-recovering GDP); the line connecting them is approximately flat or slightly upward. This makes Nigeria's slope *less* negative than the SSA average, which means β₂ becomes positive enough to flip β₁ + β₂.

The technical name for this in the spurious-regression literature is **policy-induced co-trending**. Granger and Newbold (1974, *Journal of Econometrics*) and Phillips (1986) show that when two series are jointly driven by a third trending variable (here oil revenue), within-country regressions can produce arbitrary signs.

## 5. Mechanism #2 — Why This Is Uniquely Nigerian

The natural test: do other SSA oil exporters show the same sign-flip? They don't, for three reasons.

### 5a. None had a UPE-scale single-year enrollment shock

- **Angola**: independence war 1961–1975, civil war 1975–2002. Primary GER fell from ~125 (mid-1970s) to ~50 (1990) — but this trajectory is dominated by *war*, not policy, so it is not synchronized with oil revenue. Oil production began only in earnest after 1973 offshore discoveries, but UNITA conflict prevented any UPE-equivalent expansion.
- **Gabon**: oil discovered 1970; OPEC member 1975–95 and again from 2016. Primary GER was already > 100% by 1970 (a French colonial legacy of universal primary schooling), so there was no enrollment expansion to synchronize with the oil shock — the within-country variation in OOSC is small.
- **Republic of Congo**: oil revenue rose from 1973 with first global shock; civil wars 1993–94 and 1997. Same story as Angola — civil conflict overrode any oil-funded education channel.
- **Cameroon**: oil discovered 1977, exports started 1980 — i.e., *after* the boom-finance window for UPE-style expansion. GER was a steady upward trend through the 1970s and 80s with no single discontinuity.

Nigeria was unique in having (i) oil revenue at high levels by 1973, (ii) a federal government that captured 80% of oil revenue (Decree 6, 1975), (iii) a deliberately announced national UPE program in 1976, (iv) implementation across a very large primary-age population, and (v) catastrophic fiscal collapse 1981–86 that reversed the gains.

### 5b. None had Nigeria's oil-share-in-GDP / fiscal-share-in-government combination

By 1980 oil = 22% of Nigerian GDP but **80% of government revenue** and **96% of export earnings**. This means oil-price shocks transmit into education spending almost 1-for-1, with no diversification cushion. Pinto (1987) shows that Indonesia, with comparable oil dependence, used non-oil exchange-rate management and agricultural protection to insulate education spending; Nigeria did not. This is the mechanism Sala-i-Martin and Subramanian (2013) formalize: in countries with poor institutional quality, oil-price shocks pass through to public-good provision (including primary education) with very high elasticity, generating exactly the (GER, GDP) co-trending you see in your data.

### 5c. Nigeria's military regimes were exceptionally extractive

Bhattacharyya and Hodler (2010, *European Economic Review* 54(4): 608–621) show that the resource-curse–institutions interaction is amplified when democratic accountability is weak. Nigeria 1970–1999 had ≈ 4 years of civilian rule out of 30. The Bevan-Collier-Gunning (1999) comparison with Indonesia is decisive: Indonesia under Suharto was also non-democratic but had stronger fiscal institutions and routed oil revenue into a continuous, policy-stable rural development program (BIMAS, INPRES) that *did* deliver a smooth education trajectory. Nigeria's military regimes routed oil revenue into one-shot programs (UPE 1976, Festac 1977, Ajaokuta Steel) followed by collapse.

This combination — **resource-curse fiscal transmission × weak institutions × discrete oil-price boom-bust × federal monopoly of oil revenue × short-horizon military rule × specific-year UPE policy shock** — is what produces the within-country (GER, GDP) co-trending in 1970–99 Nigeria, and what makes Nigeria's empirical pattern essentially incomparable to the rest of SSA over that window.

## 6. Mechanism #3 — Pre-1990 Enrollment-Data Quality Concerns

Even if the above mechanisms were absent, the WDI Nigerian primary GER series 1970–99 is not of comparable quality to 2000+. Three specific concerns.

### 6a. Missing observations clustered in the regimes that mattered

In your own dataset, WDI `SE.PRM.ENRR` is missing for **1974, 1975** (the immediate pre-UPE baseline); for **1997, 1998** (the late Abacha years); and is partially missing in earlier sequences. The pre-2000 sequence relies heavily on UNESCO interpolation between sparse Federal Ministry of Education submissions, with submission gaps coinciding with the periods of greatest political instability. UNESCO's own *Education for All 2015 National Review* and the 2014 UNESCO/UIS Global Initiative on Out-of-School Children Nigeria study both note that "the system of collecting comprehensive, relevant data for planning is weak in Nigeria's education sector."

### 6b. GER overshoot is itself a sign of administrative reporting problems

Reported GER values of 110.6 (1982), 110.3 (1983), and 109.1 (1984) imply that more children were enrolled in primary school than existed in the primary-age (typically 6–11) population. Mechanically this is possible (it is what GER is *designed* to allow — over-age and under-age enrollment) but the magnitude — over 110% nationally — strongly suggests administrative inflation by state governments seeking federal allocation. Pre-UBEC (i.e., before the 2004 establishment of the Universal Basic Education Commission and its National Personnel Audits), enrollment counts were submitted by state ministries with no central audit. The 1985 UNESCO/IBE Nigeria country study and the 1990 World Bank *Nigeria: Public Expenditure on Education* report both flag these state-reported numbers as upward-biased.

### 6c. Population denominator revisions

GER's denominator is the UN Population Division's primary-age population estimate. Nigerian primary-age population grew from ~12M (1970) to ~32M (2000), but **the official UN estimates of this denominator have been revised substantially in successive World Population Prospects releases (1994, 2002, 2008, 2017, 2022)**, especially for the 1970s–80s. Revisions arise because Nigeria's last fully completed census before 2006 was 1963; the 1973, 1991, and 2006 censuses had contested totals. Lincove (2009, *Economics of Education Review* 28(4): 474–484) — using the same WDI data — explicitly notes that pre-1990 Nigerian schooling determinants are unreliable for time-series analysis at the national level. When the denominator is revised, the entire GER series (and therefore your OOSC = 100 − GER) is implicitly re-anchored, but the revisions are not retrospectively pushed through every WDI vintage. Different WDI download dates therefore give materially different pre-2000 numbers.

### 6d. The 2015 admin error in your own data

Your downloaded series has GER = 25.6% for 2015 — clearly a Federal Ministry of Education or UIS administrative error, since 2014 = 84.1 and 2016 = 78.9. If a one-year admin error this large can survive into the current WDI vintage, the pre-2000 data — collected under SAP austerity, under three military regimes, with no UBEC audit — should be treated with much more skepticism. (You should drop or interpolate the 2015 observation in any final estimation.)

The Lee and Lee (2016, *Journal of Development Economics* 122: 147–169) historical schooling dataset offers an alternative pre-2000 series, but Lee-Lee themselves caution that pre-1990 SSA enrollment is the weakest sub-region of their reconstruction, with imputation rates exceeding 50% for several countries including Nigeria.

## 7. Implication for the Paper

### Why restricting to 2000+ is the right empirical choice

1. **Sample-period homogeneity.** 2000+ is a single political regime (continuous civilian rule), a single education-policy regime (1999 UBE Act in force), a single international reporting regime (post-Education-for-All Dakar 2000 monitoring; post-2004 UBEC National Personnel Audits), and uses post-2006-census-anchored UN population denominators. The within-Nigeria variation in OOSC over 2000–24 is policy-meaningful (Boko Haram, COVID, post-2014 fiscal contraction) rather than oil-fiscal-mechanical.

2. **The 1970–99 sign-flip is not a robustness "finding" — it is a confirmation of the resource-curse human-capital channel.** Frame it that way. Gylfason (2001, *European Economic Review* 45(4–6): 847–859) predicts exactly this: in resource-driven economies, the cyclical comovement of human-capital indicators with GDP is dominated by the resource cycle, not by the production-function channel. Cockx and Francken (2016, *Energy Policy* 92: 394–408) directly test and find that resource rents do not raise SSA education spending — and Nigeria 1970–99 is the textbook illustration. Stijns (2006, *World Development* 34(6): 1060–1083) provides cross-country panel evidence consistent with this.

3. **The pre-2000 data are not interpretable through your theoretical model.** Your specification (Δlog GDP_pc ≈ a + b·OOSC + Solow controls) is an augmented Solow / MRW-style production-function regression. It implicitly assumes a stable institutional and policy environment, in which OOSC reflects within-country policy variation orthogonal to GDP shocks. That assumption is true 2000–24 (the variance in Nigerian OOSC is driven by Boko Haram, demographic transition, and state-level reforms like Lagos's EKOEXCEL — none directly driven by GDP). It is decisively *false* 1970–99, when both OOSC and GDP are driven by oil-price-and-regime shocks.

### How to frame this in Section 5.4 of the paper

> *"We restrict the empirical sample to 2000–2024 because the 1970–1999 Nigerian record is dominated by three policy-induced co-trending episodes: the oil-funded 1976 Universal Primary Education expansion (during which primary GER rose from 49% to over 110% in eight years while oil-financed GDP per capita rose ~30%); the 1981–1986 oil bust and 1986 Structural Adjustment Programme (during which both primary GER and GDP per capita contracted by ~25%); and the 1985–1998 military-regime austerity that compressed both education spending and GDP. In a two-way fixed-effects specification, these episodes generate a within-Nigeria covariance between OOSC and GDP per capita growth that is dominated by oil-fiscal transmission rather than by a human-capital production-function channel. The Nigeria-specific interaction term β₂ is correspondingly identified off the difference between Nigeria's oil-driven within-country slope and the SSA-pooled slope, producing a positive estimated β₁ + β₂ when pre-2000 data are included. We interpret this not as evidence against the human-capital hypothesis but as confirmation of the resource-curse human-capital channel of Gylfason (2001) and Cockx and Francken (2016), in which oil-rich economies with weak institutions exhibit education-growth dynamics dominated by the resource cycle. The 2000–2024 sample isolates the post-civilian-transition policy regime, in which OOSC variation reflects state-level reforms, demographic transition, and the Boko Haram conflict (Bertoni, Di Maio, Molini, and Nisticò, 2019), and yields the theoretically expected negative Nigeria-specific coefficient (β₁ + β₂ = −0.137, p < 0.001)."*

That paragraph defends the sample restriction in a way that turns a methodological choice into a substantive contribution.
