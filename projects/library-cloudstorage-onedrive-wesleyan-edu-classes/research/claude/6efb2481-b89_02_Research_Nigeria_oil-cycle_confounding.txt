## Claim 6: Demographic Transition / Why Out-of-School Decline Is Independent of GDP

**10. Nwoke, M. B., et al. (2024). "Assessing the phenomenon of out-of-school children in Nigeria: Issues, gaps and recommendations." *Review of Education* (BERA / Wiley), 12(1).**
- Venue: peer-reviewed education journal (British Educational Research Association)
- Use it for: documents the structural, non-macroeconomic drivers of Nigeria's out-of-school rates — regional/state-level disparities (a 15-percentage-point primary enrollment gap between northern and southern zones), gender gap (~20 pp), distance-to-school, and household poverty — and explicitly frames the policy response as state-level reform rather than aggregate-growth-driven.
- URL: https://bera-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rev3.70011

**11. Lincove, J. A. (2009). "Determinants of schooling for boys and girls in Nigeria under a policy of free primary education." *Economics of Education Review*, 28(4), 474–484.**
- Venue: *Economics of Education Review* (Elsevier; the leading economics-of-education journal)
- Use it for: peer-reviewed empirical evidence that household-level and locational determinants (urban vs. rural residence, household wealth, parental education, distance to school) — not aggregate GDP — drive Nigerian primary enrollment. Lincove finds children in the wealthiest quintile are "seven to nine times more likely to be enrolled than those from the poorest quintile" and that distance to school is a binding constraint.
- URL (gateway): https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/economics-of-education-review

**Optional supplementary urbanization citation:**
**Henderson, J. V., & Turner, M. A. (2020). "Urbanization in the Developing World: Too Early or Too Slow?" *NBER Working Paper No. 27201.*** Use it for the broader urbanization–human-capital channel argument (urban residence is associated with substantially higher schooling attainment in SSA).
- URL: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w27201/w27201.pdf

- **The "8% 2020 contraction" figure is too large.** Realized 2020 Nigerian real GDP contraction per the World Bank dataset was approximately **−1.8%** (Q2 alone was −6.1% y/y). The −8% appears to be either a forecast scenario or a confusion with another country. I'd recommend citing the World Bank data series directly and using "approximately −1.8% in 2020, with Q2 contracting by 6.1% year-on-year."

For "50–70% of government revenue," the IMF Article IV reports support figures in this range historically, though the exact number fluctuates with oil prices. I'd cite the IMF Article IV reports as the authoritative source.
- **For the EKOEXCEL "1,009 schools" figure**, the EKOEXCEL/NewGlobe own materials are the only source; the Kremer et al. paper validates the *methodology* but was conducted in Kenya, not Lagos. Be precise in your paper about this distinction — cite Gray-Lobe et al.
