**Patterns that emerge across all 34 examples:**

- The most senior people use the **shortest** bios. Nat Friedman's homepage has *no bio* — just his name. Daniel Gross uses six one-line sentences, each a single fact. Patrick Collison says he *"works at Stripe"* — that's it. Gravitas comes from understatement, not stacking.
- People with something to prove (Julian Shapiro, Wes Bos, Lee Robinson) write more — full paragraphs, multiple "previously" clauses, named clients/companies as proof.
- Female and African founders in the encyclopedic register lead with **name + nationality + entrepreneur + the one company they're known for**. Then awards. Then the rest. Nobody opens with school.
- "Passionate about X" is the standard mission-led opener in African VC bios (Future Africa). It works because it's followed immediately by hard numbers ("$2 billion across 50 million transactions").

## Part B — Five candidate taglines for Naomi (with tradeoffs)

Each follows a different register from the corpus above. Naomi's first name + last name appear above the tagline in all cases.

### 1. Editorial (Sahil/Reid/swyx style) — three nouns, full stop

> **Founder. Builder. Student.**

Or, more precise:

> **Founder, Sabi. Student, Wesleyan.**

**Tradeoff.** Cleanest, most authoritative. Reads like Reid Hoffman's *"Entrepreneur. Investor. Strategist."* — no verbs, no claims of quality, just roles. Best for funders/VCs because it lets the work speak. Risk: feels a little cold for a young founder; doesn't tell a journalist *what Sabi is*. Mitigated by having a one-sentence "what" directly below.

### 2. Mission-led (Trinity Mouzon / Julian Shapiro / Krouse style) — "I build X"

> **I build voice AI that teaches Nigerian children to read on $5 phones.**

**Tradeoff.** This is the single strongest line in the document for grant funders and journalists — it pitches the work, the geography, the constraint, and the population in 13 words. Mirrors *"I build Val Town, a delightful way to write and deploy code in the cloud"* exactly. Risk: VCs sometimes recoil from copy that sounds nonprofit-coded ("$5 phones" reads as social-impact rather than market). Mitigated by what comes underneath (TAM, growth numbers).

### 3. Identity-led (Aboyeji / Eweniyi Wikipedia register) — third-person, encyclopedic

> **Naomi Ivie is a Nigerian entrepreneur. She is the founder of Sabi, a voice AI tutor for children across Nigeria.**

**Tradeoff.** This is the most "press release"-ready and prints clean in any article ever written about her. Same DNA as the Wikipedia leads for Aboyeji, Eweniyi, Murati, Wojcicki. Risk: third-person on a personal site can read as someone who copy-pasted their LinkedIn header — unless paired with first-person prose immediately below. Best to keep this as the *press-bio* version on a separate "/about" or "/press" section, not the hero.

### 4. Action-led (mission + scale + specificity)

> **Building Sabi — a voice tutor for the 20 million children Nigeria's schools can't reach.**

**Tradeoff.** This is the line a hackathon judge will remember. It has the only thing copy guides agree on — *specificity wins*: a real number, a named country, a real problem. Same shape as Aboyeji's *"prosperity and purpose within reach for all Africans"* but harder-edged. Risk: the 20M figure has to be defensible in every conversation that follows. (It is — out-of-school + functionally illiterate children in Nigeria comfortably clears 20M per the project's own research files.)

### 5. Quiet-confidence (Krouse / Friedman style) — understatement

> **I make things. Currently: Sabi.**

**Tradeoff.** This is the most senior-coded line — it's the move someone with twelve products and three exits makes. For a 21-year-old it can read as either *very confident* or *evasive*, depending on whether the work below it backs it up. Naomi's project list (five shipped products, $10K UNICEF cohort, Wesleyan New Venture, MIT 2nd place) probably backs it up. Risk: a journalist or grant reviewer who lands on this page cold has no idea who she is yet. **Use only if every project card underneath has clear, hard descriptions.**

**My pick if Naomi wants one answer:** option 2 ("I build voice AI that teaches Nigerian children to read on $5 phones.") because it's the only one that *also functions as a one-line pitch in a grant cover letter*. The editorial version (option 1) goes underneath as a sub-line: *Founder, Sabi · Wesleyan '27*.

## Part E — Recognition section copy

The corpus is clear: people who list awards as a vertical bullet list always read more junior than people who weave awards into prose. The Aboyeji Future-Africa bio is the model: mission sentence → company achievements with numbers → awards as a comma-separated clause inside a sentence.

**Recommended structure:** call this section *"Recognition"* (not "Awards" or "Accomplishments"), one paragraph of running prose, then a small list of dated honors below for scanners.

### Sample paragraph (45 words)

> Sabi has been recognized by UNICEF (AI Ventures inaugural cohort, $10K), MIT (2nd place, Africa Business Challenge, 2026), Wesleyan (New Venture Grand Prize), and the Schmidt Futures × Rhodes Trust Rise program. Earlier work was supported by the Patricelli Center for Social Entrepreneurship and a $360K African Scholars merit scholarship.

### Sample below-the-fold list (clean format)

> 2026 — MIT Sloan Africa Business Challenge, 2nd place
> 2026 — UNICEF AI Ventures, inaugural cohort
> 2025 — Wesleyan New Venture, Grand Prize
> 2025 — Patricelli Center Social Impact Fellow
> 2025 — Schmidt Futures × Rhodes Trust Rise, Finalist
> 2024 — Morgan Hacks, 1st place
> 2024 — Tuck Bridge Program, Dartmouth
> 2023 — African Leadership Academy, Valedictorian · Best in Economics
> 2023 — $360K African Scholars Merit Scholarship, Wesleyan

**Rules I followed:**

- Year on the left. Date-anchored awards read as fact; undated ones read as résumé padding.
- Grants/fellowships first, hackathon wins after, school honors last. Funder credibility outranks student credibility.
- Don't restate the prize money on every line. The $10K UNICEF figure goes in the paragraph once; the line below just says "inaugural cohort."
- "Wesleyan New Venture, Grand Prize" — not "won." The award name + "Grand Prize" *is* the verb. (The articles I pulled on awards-without-bragging all converge on this: name the recognition, don't narrate the winning.)
- Don't put *valedictorian* on a separate gold line. It goes in 2023, sentence-cased, with ALA's name next to it.

## Part G — Voice rules for the site (10 rules)

These are derived from the patterns that recur in the verbatim corpus, weighted toward the senior-coded sites (Friedman, Gross, Collison, Hoffman, Krouse).

1. **No exclamation marks. Anywhere on the site.** The entire corpus of 30+ founder pages I read contained essentially zero. Exclamation marks are a junior tell.
2. **Present tense for ongoing work, past tense only for shipped-and-shelved.** "I build Sabi," not "I'm building." "Sabi is live in Lagos," not "I have launched Sabi in Lagos."
3. **Numerals for any number 10 or above, and any number with a unit.** "$10K," "$5 phones," "20 million children," "2nd place." (Spell out one through nine if no unit follows.)
4. **No adjective superlatives.** No "innovative," "first," "only," "huge," "best," "groundbreaking," "revolutionary." The search results were emphatic: *"these words signal inexperience."* If the thing is best, the data says so.
5. **No "homework project" tells.** Never "I made this in 48 hours / for a class / to learn X." If it shipped, treat it as a product. If it didn't ship, don't list it.
6. **First-person for the hero, third-person only in a dedicated press bio.** Mixing them on the same page (the Murillo / Brown HubSpot pattern) reads as confused. Pick one for the homepage.
7. **One company per sentence in the awards section.** "MIT Africa Business Challenge, 2nd place" — not "2nd place winner at the MIT-Sloan-organized Africa Business Challenge held in Cambridge in 2026."
8. **Name the hard part of each project.** Krouse names "write and deploy code in the cloud." Gross names "compute," "search," "AI program at Y-Combinator." Skip the hard part and the project reads as a wrapper; name it and it reads as engineering.
9. **Geography earns its place when it's a constraint, not when it's identity decoration.** "Nigerian children" is load-bearing for Sabi (audience, language, hardware, regulation). "From Lagos" alone in a tagline is decoration. Use geography where it changes what the work has to do.
10. **One concrete operational fact per page.** "Hosted on a GPU server in Helsinki." "Runs in Pidgin." "Toll-free number through Africa's Talking." Concrete details function like footnotes — they say *I have actually built this*. The corpus's most credible builders (Krouse, Shapiro, Robinson) put exactly one of these per section.

### Bonus rules specific to the Sabi/funder question raised in the brief

11. **Christian / Spirit-filled identity stays off the professional site.** Bethel can mention "devotional" and "Bible" because *the product is for that*. Nowhere else. UNICEF / Gates / Mastercard reviewers are sensitive to faith-coded language even when they shouldn't be.
12. **"Nigerian" leads in the bio, not in the tagline.** The Aboyeji / Eweniyi / Wojcicki / Murati Wikipedia pattern is consistent: nationality is the first descriptor *after* the name in the press bio, and the work is what's named in the tagline. Reversing this — "Nigerian founder building…" as the tagline — works for journalists but slightly underclubs the work.
13. **Can one bio serve grant funders, VCs, and journalists?** Mostly yes, if it follows the encyclopedic pattern (Bio B above) on the hero and a longer Bio A is one click away on /about. The "I build X for Y" tagline + name-stack sub-line covers all three audiences because each finds what they need in the same six lines: VCs read the company name, funders read the population, journalists read the story.
