## PART A: BOARD MEETINGS FOR EARLY-STAGE NONPROFITS

### A1. The First Board Meeting — What Must Happen (BoardSource canonical)

BoardSource is unambiguous about what the first meeting of a founding board MUST cover. From the BoardSource "Building a Nonprofit Board for the First Time" page (verbatim/near-verbatim extraction):

**Required first-meeting agenda items:**
- "Fix the name and the legal address for the organization to be included in its legal documents"
- "Elect officers"
- "Authorize new officers to make business decisions for the organization such as opening bank accounts, signing checks, signing a lease"
- "Adopt a set of bylaws, if already drafted, or start creating this document"
- "Implement terms and term limits to allow your board to refresh itself and bring in new ideas on a regular basis"
- "Assign duties for everyone"

**BoardSource on founding-board composition (verbatim):**
- "A founding board is critical to a new organization's start and implementing an organization's vision."
- BoardSource explicitly **warns against adding friends** as founding board members — founders "often regret" this approach.
- "Recruit people who understand your mission area and are familiar with your constituents and their needs."
- "A board that is homogeneous in any way risks its ability to make the best decisions and plans" — "racial and ethnic diversity is particularly concerning" if absent.
- "All board members must be willing and able to commit the time needed to set up the organization." During startup, if there is no staff, "the board must also serve in that capacity."

**Where BoardSource says to find members:**
- Personal & professional networks
- Affinity groups
- Other nonprofits
- Professional associations
- Major corporations & community outreach programs
- Local businesses
- Nonprofit support orgs, United Way chapters, community foundations
- News/printed media featuring community leaders
- Local board matching programs

**How the board accomplishes work (verbatim):**
- "Create committees or task forces to accomplish the board's work and engage your board members."
- "Start with a governance committee that is tasked with renewing the board and educating it."
- Governance committee should: "Orient new board members," "Train and develop the board as to their role… It is important the board understands the work of the organization as well as the role of the board."
- "Emphasize that boards act only as a collective entity — decision-making is a group function."

### A2. The Standard Agenda Structure (multi-source consensus)

The canonical first-meeting agenda combining BoardSource, Boardable, Zeffy, and Donorbox guidance:

1. **Call to Order** — formal opening; recorded time
2. **Roll Call / Confirm Quorum** — note in minutes
3. **Introductions** (especially critical for first meeting — bios, what each member brings)
4. **Mission Recap & Strategic Context** (founder grounds the room in the "why")
5. **Approval of Prior Minutes** (N/A first meeting — instead, approve incorporation docs)
6. **Founder/Executive Report** — what's been built, what's next, key risks, decisions needed
7. **Financial Report / Budget Review** — even pre-revenue; show cash position + projected burn
8. **Officer Reports** (Chair, Treasurer, Secretary — after officers are elected)
9. **Committee Reports** (for first meeting: propose initial committees)
10. **Unfinished Business** (N/A first meeting)
11. **New Business** — votes & decisions: bylaws, officers, banking authority, fiscal year, conflict-of-interest policy adoption, IRS/CAC compliance
12. **Action Items** — assigned to specific people with deadlines
13. **Next Meeting** date/location set before adjournment
14. **Adjournment**

**Key timing rule (multi-source):** "Give each agenda item a set time frame. Sticking to the timeframes on the agenda consistently shows board and committee members that they, and their time, are valued."

**Decisions/Action Items framework:** "Ensure that action items are assigned to responsible individuals and that deadlines are clearly defined, and follow up between meetings to track progress."

### A3. Pre-Meeting Briefing Packet — What's Standard

**Distribution timing:** "A pre-meeting board packet distributed 5-7 days ahead of time." Best practice: send the agenda "a month before" first meeting; final packet 7 days out; minimum 72 hours.

**Length rule:** "Best practice recommends keeping materials concise with a board packet **under 20 pages**."

**Standard contents:**
- Agenda (with time blocks)
- Prior meeting minutes (or for first meeting: incorporation docs)
- **Financials**: executive summary of recent + near-term projected figures, KPIs, "new and lost accounts, revenue trends, cash flow, quarterly totals"; exhibits include P&L, balance sheet, cash-flow statements
- **Management Discussion & Analysis (MD&A)**: "succinct commentary on recent and projected performance, presenting the challenges that are keeping the CEO or Executive Director up at night along with progress toward short- and long-term goals"
- Executive/founder report
- Proposals, motions, or resolutions requiring a vote
- Committee reports
- Attendee list (board + any guests/presenters, with note if guest is partial-attendance)
- Strategic discussion materials
- For founding meeting specifically: **bylaws draft, articles of incorporation, EIN/CAC docs, draft conflict-of-interest policy, draft board member agreement, statement of expectations**

### A4. Meeting Frequency — Pre-Pilot Norms

**Quarterly is most common for mature nonprofits.** "Most nonprofit boards meet quarterly, which allows them to stay updated on operations and make timely decisions."

**Monthly is appropriate during critical periods.** "Some nonprofits may benefit from monthly meetings, especially during critical periods, such as fundraising campaigns or strategic planning sessions." "If you go longer than a quarter without meeting, it will be more difficult to stay on task and achieve meeting goals since much of the meeting will be a recap."

**Startup-stage funding parallel (useful frame):**
- Seed-stage companies typically meet monthly
- Series A startups transition from monthly to quarterly
- Series B+ generally settle into quarterly

**Recommendation for Sabi at current stage (pre-pilot, founder-led, board recently assembled):** **Bi-monthly (every 6–8 weeks) for the first 6 months, then quarterly** once pilot is live and routines are stable. Rationale: pre-pilot phase has too many decisions for quarterly; monthly is excessive volunteer time for advisory board members like Hon. Folasade and Barrister Ogunlewe; bi-monthly hits the middle.

**Counter-view to know:** Joan Garry has written "Your Monthly Board Meeting is a Waste of Time" — argues monthly cadence forces boards into management territory and prevents strategic focus. Worth reading before locking a cadence.

### A5. Quorum & Voting Norms

**Quorum (BoardSource + state-law range):**
- **Most common threshold: simple majority (more than 50%)** of total board members
- **State minimums often allow as low as one-third** (some states mandate this floor for nonprofits)
- "Some bylaws require a majority present for routine matters and a supermajority for fundamental changes"
- Common options orgs codify: 33%, 51% (simple majority), 60%, 66.67% (two-thirds), 75%

**Voting thresholds:**
- Routine matters: simple majority of those present (quorum being met)
- Bylaw amendments, removal of board member, dissolution: supermajority typically 67%–90%

**BoardSource warning:** "Some boards choose a high number, such as 100 percent, to ensure full participation. In many cases, this backfires, and boards find that quorum is never reached." Set it where attendance is realistic.

**For a 7-person board:** Quorum at simple majority = 4 members. Recommend codifying 4-of-7 quorum in bylaws. For amendments, recommend 5-of-7 (two-thirds).

**Documentation:** "Recording quorum status in meeting minutes, noting whether a quorum was present and actions taken."

### A6. Handling Slow / Unresponsive Volunteer Board Members (the LIVE issue)

This is well-covered in nonprofit governance literature. Direct guidance from the research:

**Step 1 — Set explicit expectations BEFORE the problem.**
"Give board members a clear understanding of what's expected of them, with many organizations using a **statement of expectations or even a signed agreement** to make responsibilities explicit. Provide a board job description that clearly and plainly describes the expectations of board members—if board members are expected to raise funds, say so; if meeting attendance is required, be clear about this."

**Step 2 — Build accountability systems.**
"Accountability systems—like a shared spreadsheet to track tasks—make it easy to confirm commitments, monitor progress, and step in when tasks fall behind."

**Step 3 — Board Chair acts FAST.**
"The Board Chair should act without delay to inform a board member when their participation is close to falling below the nonprofit's requirements, including a phone call to the member who has missed successive meetings."

**Step 4 — Diagnose root cause.**
"Reliability increases when your needs align with their skills and passions." Often, unresponsiveness = mis-fit between assignment and the person's actual interest/capacity, not bad character.

**Step 5 — Use term renewal as a natural off-ramp.**
"The simple process of having to renew a term will often provide a board member struggling with the responsibilities of board membership the right moment to step away in good standing; if an under-performing board member doesn't volunteer to step down, the board development committee must acknowledge that renewing another term would not be in the best interest of the board member or the organization."

**Step 6 — Last resort: removal.**
"Bylaws often explicitly provide that **failure to attend a certain number of meetings is cause for removal**, since that board member's absence inequitably burdens fellow board members and makes it difficult for the absent member to fulfill their fiduciary duties." "Some states permit removal of board members with or without cause, whereas other states permit removal only with cause." Under Nigeria's CAMA 2020, the constitution must provide procedures for "appointment, removal and replacement of trustees."

**Feedback science:** "A board member who is informed that they have attended only 20 percent of the past year's board meetings is receiving useful feedback on performance."

**Term-limit standard:** "The most common board member term structure is **two consecutive three-year terms**."

**For Naomi specifically (slow board recruitment because mom is over-extended):**
- This is a symptom of "founder-syndrome adjacent" board formation — a board hand-picked through one channel (mom's network).
- SSIR/Joan Garry warning: "The seeds for founder syndrome are often planted not because of the founder but because of the relatively thoughtless structure built around them. She describes how boards sometimes form with members hand-selected by the founder, which creates dependency and lack of independent governance."
- **Action**: take ownership of board recruitment yourself; create a **governance committee** (per BoardSource) whose first task is filling remaining seats; use the Bridgespan three-table method (below) to identify skill gaps and recruit against them, not against your mom's address book.

### A7. Sub-Committees — How They Actually Work

**Definition (BoardSource/Boardable):** "A board committee is a small, mission-focused workgroup — made up of directors, staff, and sometimes subject-matter volunteers — that researches issues, proposes solutions, and, where authorized, makes decisions on behalf of the board."

**Two types:**
- **Standing committees** = permanent, meet regularly (Finance, Governance, Audit, Program/Curriculum, Fundraising)
- **Task forces / Ad hoc** = formed for one purpose, dissolved on completion (e.g., "First-Pilot Launch Task Force")

**Audit Committee — specific structure:**
- "Usually a small committee of three to five members"
- Work "is often seasonal, tied to the end of the association's fiscal year"
- Responsibilities: "manage the audit process, select the independent auditor, review the audit report with the auditor, present the report to the full board, use the independent auditor as an advisor, and ensure compliance with all reporting requirements"
- **Independence rule:** "The committee would not be independent if someone who is employed by the nonprofit or audit firm served on the committee"

**Governance Committee** — BoardSource calls this the #1 committee a new nonprofit must form. Tasks: orient new board members, develop board training, manage term limits, lead board self-evaluation, identify recruitment needs.

**For Sabi's pre-pilot stage, recommended initial committee structure:**
1. **Governance Committee** (2–3 members) — onboarding, bylaws, recruitment
2. **Finance & Audit Committee** (2 members + Treasurer) — budget oversight, audit prep, financial controls
3. **Curriculum / Pedagogy Committee** (2–3 members) — could include external educator-volunteers, not just board; reviews lesson content, learning-science alignment, partnership with EKOEXCEL/NALABE
4. **Fundraising / Partnerships Committee** (2 members) — grant strategy, donor cultivation; defer until post-pilot if too thin now

Note: research did not surface a standardized "curriculum committee" template — they exist commonly in school boards and education-focused nonprofits but each defines them locally. For Sabi this would be the committee where the academic/pedagogical members (Hon. Folasade if education-policy, plus external educators) drive content review.

### A8. Nigerian / African Nonprofit Governance — What's Different from US Norms

**CAMA 2020 (the governing statute):**
- NGOs register as **Incorporated Trustees under Part F**
- **Minimum 2 trustees required** (vs US norm of 3+)
- Per Section 827, the constitution must list "names, addresses, and occupations of all trustees"
- Constitution MUST include: name, objectives, subscriptions/contributions, procedure for disbursement of funds, accounting & auditing provisions, AND "provisions for the appointment, removal and replacement of trustees and the duties and powers of the trustees"
- **Section 845: bi-annual statements to the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC) required** for financial transparency
- The CAC can intervene in mismanagement cases — "power to suspend trustees or appoint interim managers"

**Operational implications for Sabi:**
1. Bylaws/constitution must explicitly state trustee removal mechanism — this is mandatory under CAMA 2020, not optional
2. Bi-annual CAC filings are a hard compliance deadline
3. The trustee structure under CAMA is closer to the US "board of directors" model — same fiduciary weight
4. Many Nigerian nonprofits keep separate **advisory boards** (no legal authority, no fiduciary liability) alongside their Incorporated Trustees — useful if Hon. Folasade or Barrister Ogunlewe want to advise without legal exposure
5. Nigerian advisory boards often include senior/honorific figures (Hons., Barristers, traditional rulers) where formal voting expectations would be culturally awkward — advisory designation respects this

**Advisory Board vs Board of Directors/Trustees — critical distinction Naomi should clarify NOW:**
- "The nonprofit board of directors has formal legal responsibility and full decision-making authority, while advisory boards do not."
- "The board of directors has voting power, while members of the advisory board do not."
- "Unlike board directors with fiduciary duties, advisory board members don't have any liability, allowing them to freely give guidance and advice to the board."

**Is the current 7-person group an advisory board or a board of trustees?** If it's the legal Trustees under CAMA, you need: signed trustee agreements, bi-annual CAC filings, formal quorum, written removal procedures. If it's advisory, you have far more flexibility but they cannot vote on org direction. Decide this before the first meeting.

## PART B: COFOUNDER SEARCH FOR A SOLO FOUNDER (POST-MVP)

### B1. The Hardest Pill First — Read This Before Reading Anything Else

Three things the research surfaces unambiguously:

**(1) Most "late cofounders" should actually be senior hires, not cofounders.**
Direct quote from the equity-split research: "If someone joins after you've already validated the market, built an MVP, and raised funding, they are a critical early hire, not a co-founder. The recommended approach is to offer generous employee equity in the 1-3% range with an impressive title like 'Founding Engineer' or 'VP Product,' not co-founder-level equity at 30-50%."

**(2) The failure rate is real.**
Noam Wasserman research (cited across multiple sources): **"Sixty-five percent of startups fail due to founder conflict."** "Too many founders bring on a co-founder too quickly. If you really want a cofounder, don't rush it. Try to work together informally first, and be honest about your respective commitment levels."

**(3) Timing rule that may apply directly to you.**
"If it's your idea/business, leave it as long as you can before bringing in a co-founder. If you can wait until your first revenue or funding round, your business will be worth a lot more and you'll be able to bring in the same person on a much smaller percentage, or perhaps just pay them cash."

The honest framing for Naomi: you may not need a cofounder. You may need (a) an Executive Director / COO once funded, (b) a Nigeria-based country lead, (c) a Founding Engineer, or (d) a board chair who is a former operator. Each of those is different from a cofounder.

### B5. Equity Allocation When Cofounder Joins AFTER MVP

This is the single most consequential framework in this brief. Direct from the equity research:

**The "Early Hire vs Cofounder" Test:**
"If someone joins after you've already validated the market, built an MVP, and raised funding, they are a critical early hire, not a co-founder. The recommended approach is to offer generous employee equity in the 1–3% range with an impressive title like 'Founding Engineer' or 'VP Product,' not co-founder-level equity at 30–50%."

**If you decide it IS a true cofounder situation, standard frameworks:**

- **60/40 split** is common "when one cofounder quit earlier, built MVP, or brought funding"
- **90/10 split** "when one person had the idea and traction and the other joined for long-term scaling"
- **A technical cofounder joining 6 months later** typically receives **15–25%** equity (vs 40–50% if at inception)

**Pre-MVP Work Premium Framework:**
"If the technical co-founder spent 6–12 months building a functional prototype before the business co-founder joined, the key framework is to award the technical co-founder 50% base allocation plus an additional 5–15% for pre-partnership work, then split remaining equity equally going forward."

**Applied to Naomi (inverting the framing — she's the one who built):**
- Naomi has built MVP, won $3K MIT prize, deployed Chatterbox, scripted lessons, has voice clone, has Hetzner server running, has grant pipeline. That's substantial pre-cofounder traction.
- A cofounder joining now should be in the **20–35% range** at maximum, with Naomi at 65–80%
- For a NONPROFIT, equity doesn't apply directly — but compensation, title weight, decision-rights, and board seat allocation are the analogs

**Vesting (always non-negotiable):**
- **Standard: 4 years with 1-year cliff**
- "No equity vests for 12 months, then 25% vests at the cliff date, with remaining equity vesting monthly"
- For a nonprofit cofounder: time-to-decision-rights cliff — e.g., they cannot be removed for 12 months but also don't gain full board/officer authority until cliff. Protects both sides.

**Carta data point (2024 trend):** "A shift is underway in how startup co-founders split their equity" — increasingly toward 50/50 even among teams with asymmetric contributions, because conflict-avoidance value > "fairness" math. **This is the OPPOSITE of what most experts recommend post-MVP — be careful here.**

### B6. Red Flags vs Green Flags When Evaluating a Late Cofounder

**RED FLAGS (verbatim):**
- "Treating the startup like a side hustle while you're going full-time, frequently rescheduling meetings or avoiding deliverables, and hesitating when asked to leave a current job"
- "A cofounder who's burned previous business partners, ghosted teams, or left unresolved messes might repeat the pattern with you"
- "A cofounder who has rose-tinted glasses about challenges ahead, with unrealistic expectations about swift and substantial returns or exponential growth overnight"
- "Avoids hard conversations, becomes defensive during feedback, or rarely checks in and goes silent for long periods"
- "If a potential co-founder is unwilling to engage in [a] trial phase, that in itself can be a significant red flag indicating their level of commitment and suitability for the partnership"

**GREEN FLAGS (verbatim):**
- "Great co-founders bring diverse strengths to the table"
- "Partner up with someone who has a clear, realistic view of the hurdles you'll face but remains motivated and excited about overcoming them and finding innovative solutions"
- "Open, honest, and consistent communication builds trust — especially when tough decisions are on the table"

**The Trial Project Methodology:**
- Time-boxed (typically 30–90 days)
- Clear deliverables and KPIs defined upfront
- Defined weekly hour commitment (e.g., "20 hrs/week for 8 weeks")
- Explicit IP ownership terms in writing (who keeps what if it doesn't work out)
- "Schedule a meeting at the end of your trial project and define the agenda to that meeting when crafting the agreement, such as 'we will discuss whether or not we want to continue working together'"
- Both parties acknowledge in writing: "the official partnership has not yet begun"

**Founder Dating (Gloria Lin's 50-question framework):**
- 50 structured questions, takes 6–10 hours of conversation
- Don't do all at once — "covering a section or two in one sitting, with about three to five sessions total"
- Sample questions:
  - "Do you have a gut feeling about what our biggest challenge as co-founders might be?"
  - "Name three strengths or values that you believe we share."
  - "What do you value most in a professional partnership?"
  - "What, if anything, is too serious to compromise on when building a startup?"
- "You don't need to be 100% aligned on everything — 75% alignment may be acceptable. It's recommended to make a checklist of non-negotiables, but keep it small with no more than three."
- Available: First Round Review hosts the PDF; Antler has adapted to "36 Questions to Fall in Love With Your Cofounder"

### B7. Founder-Market Fit — When a Local-Nigeria Cofounder Makes Sense vs Just Hiring

This is the strategic crux of Naomi's decision.

**Case FOR adding a Nigerian cofounder (not just hiring):**
- Sabi's product is delivered IN Nigeria, in Nigerian English/Pidgin, to Nigerian children, via Nigerian telecom infrastructure. Founder-market fit gap is real.
- Equity/title-level commitment signals stronger to Nigerian partners (MTN, Airtel, ministry contacts, EKOEXCEL leadership) than "I have a country lead I hired"
- Visa/immigration: a Nigerian cofounder doesn't need US sponsorship; you don't need a Nigeria visa beyond visits
- Cultural authenticity in fundraising narrative — particularly for African-targeted funders (Mastercard Foundation, Co-Impact Africa, Acumen West Africa)
- Long-term Nigeria entity structure (likely needed under CAMA 2020) is much cleaner with a Nigerian principal

**Case AGAINST cofounder, FOR hiring Nigeria-based COO/Country Director:**
- You give up ~20–35% of decision rights / a board seat permanently to mitigate a risk that a high-quality Country Director ($30–60K/year in Lagos) solves
- Faster: hire in 2–3 months vs cofounder evaluation 6+ months
- Reversible: a bad hire can be replaced; a bad cofounder is a multi-year disentanglement
- 65% founder-conflict failure rate is the dominant statistic
- Many Nigerian edtech founders (uLesson, Edukoya, Gradely) are SOLO Nigerian founders who hired country teams without adding cofounders later

**Reference cases (Nigerian edtech founder-led, no late cofounder):**
- **Sim Shagaya / uLesson** — solo founder, raised $15M Series B (2021)
- **Honey Ogundeyi / Edukoya** — solo founder (former UK-Nigeria Tech Hub Director)
- **Boye Oshinaga / Gradely** — solo founder, scaled to 70 schools

These are existence-proofs that a Nigeria-based founder can scale solo with strong teams underneath. The question is whether Naomi (US-based for now) can substitute the founder-presence-in-Nigeria gap with a senior hire rather than a cofounder.

**Investor preference data point (Ingressive Capital):** "40% of the companies in their Fund I portfolio having female co-founders, and all maintaining indigenous equity ownership." Nigerian/African-focused investors often expect indigenous equity — this can be solved via local Country Director with equity grant OR via cofounder. The latter is stronger signal.

### B8. Cofounder vs Hire Executive Director / COO — The Alternative

**Framing from the research:**
"While a solo founder can bring on executives and managers to help with work and decisions, co-founders can balance out the leadership team by bringing different areas of expertise, their own professional networks, and sharing responsibility."

**The decisive factor (most-cited expert position):**
"If you've already developed a concept, it may make more sense to secure seed funding and then hire strong people to round out the team. If it's your idea/business, leave it as long as you can before bringing in a co-founder. If you can wait until your first revenue or funding round, your business will be worth a lot more and you'll be able to bring in the same person on a much smaller percentage, or perhaps just pay them cash."

**Nonprofit-specific nuance:**
- "In nonprofit organizations, some organizations choose to hire co-executive directors. However, 'co-CEO' is not commonly used in the nonprofit world."
- "Very small, all-volunteer organizations can survive without an executive director, but growth demands central leadership and dedicated oversight."

**Co-Leadership in nonprofits — Bridgespan & SSIR research:**

Benefits: "reduce staff burnout and support work-life balance, expand options for navigating leadership transitions, incorporate multiple perspectives into decision-making, and make room for more racial, gender, and other diversity at the top."

**Critical pitfalls (verbatim):**
- "One primary risk is having co-leaders in name only — where one person holds the majority of power and authority in practice, while the secondary leader is overlooked and their efforts undermined, making them a chief executive in name only."
- "Additional risks include inefficiencies from two individuals performing duties that might otherwise be performed by one, conflicts, delays in decision-making, and opportunities lost due to those delays."
- "Without sufficient specificity on workflows and decision-making, co-led organizations can experience lower productivity, with staff experiencing confusion."

**Success requirements:**
- "Sharing the CEO role requires high levels of trust and is often based on the strength of existing relationships between leaders **before** they adopt the Co-CEO model."
- "Ideally, leaders will play distinct and complementary roles."
- "Board members and executives considering co-leadership should clarify roles, authority, and decision-making protocols at the start and follow through over time."

### B9. Founder Syndrome — The Adjacent Risk

Multiple sources flag this as the dominant board-founder governance failure mode. Joan Garry (cited heavily in the literature):

**Founder syndrome symptoms (verbatim):** "autocratic decision-making, lack of receptivity to new ideas, and leadership rooted in personal ego"

**Why it forms:** "The seeds for founder syndrome are often planted not because of the founder but because of the relatively thoughtless structure built around them. She describes how boards sometimes form with members hand-selected by the founder, which creates dependency and lack of independent governance."

**Healthy alternative model:** "A thriving nonprofit … values partnership, particularly between the board and staff, and between the staff leader and the board chair — comparing it to a twin-engine jet where each engine must function well independently and in partnership."

**Direct application to Naomi:** Board currently being assembled by her mother → high founder-syndrome risk pattern. Mitigation = ensure Board Chair is NOT mom and is NOT another close family member. Honorable Folasade or Barrister Ogunlewe in the Chair seat would create the needed independence.
