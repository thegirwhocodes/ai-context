## First: incorporation and tax-exemption are two different things

This is the piece that trips most people up, and it's probably the root of the confusion.

**CT incorporation** is a *state* action. Connecticut creates a legal person called "Education for Equality Inc." From that moment the organization exists separately from you — it can sign contracts, own property, hold a bank account, and be sued instead of you personally. That's it. Connecticut has said nothing about taxes.

**501(c)(3)** is a *federal* action, by the IRS, and it's a completely separate application (Form 1023-EZ, $275). It says: this corporation is exempt from federal income tax, **and** donations to it are tax-deductible for the donor.

So you can be incorporated and not tax-exempt. That's a real, normal state to be in — it just means the door to most foundation grants and TechSoup/Google-style nonprofit programs is still shut, because those all ask for the IRS **determination letter**, not the certificate of incorporation. Worth checking which of the two you're holding.

## The four entities, and what each one is for

Your roadmap describes four entities. Only two of them are meant to exist now.

**1. Education for Equality Inc. — Connecticut nonstock corporation (US)**

This is the *parent in spirit*, and the IP owner. It holds the copyright in the 286 lesson scripts, the curriculum, the Sabi code, the trademark. It receives grants and donations. It doesn't run programs on the ground in Nigeria — it funds them.

"Nonstock" means it has no shareholders. Nobody owns it. It's controlled by its **board of directors**, not by an owner — which is why the roadmap insists on 3 directors and why "no private inurement" matters: since nobody owns it, nobody is allowed to extract profit from it.

**2. Education for Equality Nigeria — Incorporated Trustee (Nigeria, CAC)**

An Incorporated Trustee is Nigeria's version of a charity/NGO structure. Same logic as nonstock: no owners, no shares. Instead of directors it has **trustees**, who hold the organization's assets *in trust* for its stated charitable objects — the eight object clauses in your filing packet (free literacy/numeracy, voice-based delivery, TaRL-style remediation, partnerships, field workers, non-exploitative data, receiving grants).

This is the entity that legally operates in Nigeria: signs agreements with Lagos schools, employs or contracts field staff, and can receive Nigerian government grants. Without it, you have no legal standing to run programs there.

**3. EfE Nigeria Ltd — a for-profit company (future, Phase 3)**

Only if you ever *sell* something — tutoring to private schools. Pays ~29% Nigerian tax.

**4. Delaware C-Corp (future, Phase 4)**

Only if you ever raise venture capital. Nonprofits can't issue equity, so VC money structurally cannot go into #1 or #2.

## The part that's genuinely unusual: they don't own each other

Look at the diagram again — the US entity and the Nigerian entity are drawn side by side, connected by a downward arrow, but the roadmap says explicitly of the future Ltd: *"NOT a subsidiary (no ownership link)."*

Most people assume the US org owns the Nigerian org. In your design it doesn't. And it *can't*, in the normal sense — neither entity has shares to own. They're two independent charities that happen to share a mission, a name, and some overlapping people.

What links them is **money and paperwork, not ownership**: the US 501(c)(3) makes *grants* to the Nigerian Incorporated Trustee. That's why the roadmap's key principle #1 is **expenditure responsibility** — when a US charity sends money abroad to an entity it doesn't control, the IRS requires it to document that the money was actually spent on charitable purposes. Receipts, reports, a grant agreement. That obligation exists *because* there's no ownership link.

The reason for keeping them unlinked is the future Phase 3 company: if the US nonprofit owned a Nigerian for-profit, US **CFC** (Controlled Foreign Corporation) rules would drag that Nigerian income back into the US tax net. No ownership link, no CFC problem. Your tax table lands at ~29% total instead of double taxation.

## Does the roadmap make sense?

Structurally, yes — it's the standard, correct pattern for a US charity funding foreign program work, and it hits the sequencing right (EIN → state incorporation → bylaws/board → 1023-EZ). Two real gaps in it, though:

**1. The Phase 3 licensing deal has a self-dealing problem it doesn't flag.** The roadmap has EfE Nigeria Ltd "owned by Naomi Ivie personally," licensing IP from the nonprofit at "arm's length." But Naomi is also a founder/director of the nonprofit. Under IRS rules (IRC §4958, "excess benefit transactions"), a transaction between a 501(c)(3) and an insider needs more than the word "arm's length" — it needs the *disqualified person* (Naomi) to recuse from the board vote, and the board to document comparable licensing terms *before* the deal closes, to get the legal presumption that it's reasonable. The roadmap states the goal but not the safeguard.

**2. It's missing charitable solicitation registration.** If donations get collected online nationally (not just from Connecticut), most states require registering before soliciting their residents — not just the CT incorporation. Not mentioned anywhere in the doc.

Everything else — the no-ownership-link between US and Nigerian entities, expenditure responsibility for foreign grants, the CFC-avoidance logic — is sound and matches how this is actually done.

## How large startups migrate abroad / structure to reduce tax

This is exactly the model your own roadmap sketches for Phase 4, and it's the same one Flutterwave, Paystack, and Andela all used. The mechanism is called a **flip**:

1. Founders set up a new holding company abroad — almost always **Delaware**, because that's what US VCs expect (familiar governance, SAFEs, preferred stock, QSBS eligibility).
2. The Nigerian operating company gets swapped in as a **wholly-owned subsidiary** of the Delaware holdco — founders exchange their Nigerian shares for holdco shares.
3. VC money goes into the Delaware entity, not the Nigerian one — this sidesteps Nigerian FX/capital controls and gives investors a jurisdiction with stronger contract/IP enforcement.
4. The Nigerian subsidiary keeps operating, keeps its revenue, and **still pays Nigerian corporate tax (~29-30%) on Nigerian-source income.** That part isn't erased — transfer-pricing rules require profit to be booked where the value is actually created, so you can't just shift Nigerian revenue into a zero-tax shell without real staff/operations there.

What the structure *does* reduce is US-side double taxation, not Nigerian tax: under GILTI's **high-tax exclusion**, if a foreign subsidiary's effective tax rate exceeds ~18.9% (90% of the US 21% corporate rate), its earnings are excluded from additional US tax when the Delaware parent reports them. Nigeria's ~29% CIT clears that bar — which is exactly the line your roadmap's tax table is built on.

## How did they make money before any Delaware entity existed?

Ordinary revenue — no special structure needed:

- **Paystack** is the clean example of an actual "flip." It launched in Lagos, built its payment-processing product, and billed Nigerian merchants transaction fees directly through the Lagos entity — normal commercial revenue, taxed normally in Nigeria. Only in **2015**, ahead of joining Y Combinator's W2016 batch, did they incorporate in Delaware and swap ownership into it. The flip happened *because* YC required a Delaware entity to invest in — it wasn't needed to operate or bill customers before that.
- **Flutterwave** doesn't really fit "before the flip" — its US certificate of incorporation was prepared in **May 2016**, alongside the Nigerian entity, essentially at founding. Both existed from near-day-one.
- **Andela** raised its seed round from Silicon Valley VCs "in year one," which points the same way — a US-anchored structure from very early rather than a Nigeria-only phase that later flipped.

The point that answers your question directly: **making money never required the foreign entity.** A local company sells its product to local customers, collects fees, pays local corporate tax — same as any business. The Delaware entity is a *fundraising/ownership* structure, invented for taking institutional VC money cleanly. It's not a prerequisite for revenue.

## Would flipping put your for-profit in jeopardy?

Yes, but not for the reason those examples raise — Paystack and Flutterwave owned their own product outright. Yours doesn't: per your roadmap, `EfE Nigeria Ltd` doesn't own the Sabi IP, it **licenses** it from the 501(c)(3). That's the exposure a flip creates:

1. **The license itself.** A flip swaps who owns `EfE Nigeria Ltd`'s shares — from Naomi personally into the new Delaware holdco (and eventually its VC shareholders). If the licensing agreement (Step 12 — not yet drafted) doesn't address change-of-control, the nonprofit's board would need to consent to the license continuing under new ownership. Until that's resolved, the for-profit's right to keep using the Sabi curriculum/brand is the thing actually at risk in a flip — not the flip mechanics themselves, which are routine.

2. **It cuts the other way too.** If outside VC shareholders end up with equity upside in a company running on a cheap license from a public charity, that's a private-benefit exposure *for the nonprofit's 501(c)(3) status* — the IRS could read that as the charity conferring benefit on private investors through underpriced IP.

Because there's no ownership link between the two entities today, the nonprofit itself isn't a shareholder getting diluted in a flip — its only exposure runs through that one contract. Which means the real fix, when Step 12 gets drafted, is putting explicit change-of-control and re-approval terms into the license now, rather than leaving it open for whenever a flip happens.
