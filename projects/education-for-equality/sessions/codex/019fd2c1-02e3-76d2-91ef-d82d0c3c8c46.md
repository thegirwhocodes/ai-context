## Codex · 2026-08-05T17:05:58.173Z · final_answer

A browser/WebRTC recording means capturing the person’s microphone through a webpage and the internet instead of through a telephone call.

The two paths look like this:

```text
Current phone call
Phone microphone → mobile carrier → telephone network → Africa’s Talking
→ SIP → Asterisk → 8 kHz recording → speech-to-text

Browser/WebRTC
Phone microphone → webpage → internet → Sabi server
→ 16–48 kHz recording → speech-to-text
```

The browser version preserves much more of the voice. Telephone audio generally cuts off frequencies above roughly 3.4 kHz and compresses everything into 8 kHz G.711 audio. That can blur consonants such as “th,” “t,” “s,” and “f”—exactly the distinctions causing problems in these clips.

With WebRTC:

- The caller opens a Sabi webpage on a smartphone or computer.
- They permit microphone access.
- The browser streams their voice over the internet, commonly using the higher-quality Opus codec.
- Sabi receives that stream, transcribes it and sends Sabi’s voice back through the webpage.
- We can also save a caller-only recording for testing STT models.
- Browsers usually provide echo cancellation, automatic volume control and some noise suppression.

It would probably make Oluremi’s speech much clearer to STT because it avoids the telephone network entirely. It cannot guarantee correct Nigerian-accent recognition, but it gives the model far more speech information to work with.

The trade-off is important: WebRTC requires a smartphone/computer, internet data and a browser. It therefore cannot replace Sabi’s ordinary phone-number experience for children using feature phones. It would be valuable for:

- Determining whether the problem is the phone channel or the STT model.
- Collecting clearer, consented Nigerian speech samples.
- Giving smartphone users a higher-quality Sabi option.
- Comparing the same speaker over WebRTC, the Nigerian line and the U.S. line.

In plain terms: it would be like a WhatsApp internet call embedded in Sabi’s website, rather than a normal telephone call. For our immediate question, one WebRTC recording from Oluremi saying the same seven answers would tell us how much of the failure comes from the phone line.

## Bottom line

Twilio’s US line is unlikely to make Nigerian callers materially easier to understand than an Africa’s Talking Nigerian line. It may provide better monitoring and integrations, but it does not provide higher-fidelity telephone audio.

Both paths deliver essentially the same narrowband signal:

| Route | Audio reaching the application |
|---|---|
| Africa’s Talking Nigeria | G.711 A-law/PCMA, 8 kHz, mono |
| Twilio → ElevenLabs | G.711 μ-law/PCMU, 8 kHz, mono |
| Browser/WebRTC | Typically Opus at 16–48 kHz, potentially much clearer |

Twilio explicitly sends Media Streams as μ-law, 8 kHz, mono, and ElevenLabs requires that same format for its Twilio integration. [Twilio Media Streams documentation](https://www.twilio.com/docs/voice/media-streams/websocket-messages), [ElevenLabs Twilio documentation](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-agents/phone-numbers/twilio-integration/register-call).

Our Africa’s Talking endpoint already deliberately keeps the Nigerian PCMA codec rather than unnecessarily converting it to PCMU, as shown in the [Asterisk configuration](</Users/naomiivie/Education for Equality/sabi-server/asterisk/pjsip.conf:30>). Consequently, the Asterisk hop is not obviously creating an additional lossy codec conversion.

For someone physically in the US calling the US Twilio number, Twilio may sound cleaner because the carrier route is short. But a person in Nigeria calling that US number adds an international PSTN route and potentially more carrier handoffs and transcoding. That could be equal or worse than calling an in-country Africa’s Talking number.

I found no credible controlled study showing that Nigerian speech transcribes better when routed through a US Twilio number instead of Africa’s Talking.

## Is it phone-line noise?

Partly—but ordinary background hiss is not the main problem in these clips.

From the seven difficult recordings:

- They are only 0.66–1.8 seconds long.
- Most are not clipped or overloaded.
- One “thirty” is exceptionally quiet, but several failures have reasonable volume.
- Equalization and noise reduction did not improve recognition on any of the seven clips across the tested Whisper configurations. That experiment is in [preprocess_bakeoff.py](</Users/naomiivie/Education for Equality/sabi-server/preprocess_bakeoff.py:1>).
- Google also failed badly, including inventing unrelated words. That is evidence against “we just need a larger general-purpose STT model.”

The likely causes, in order, are:

1. **The 8 kHz telephone channel.** It removes high-frequency consonant information before we ever receive the audio. Upsampling to 16 or 48 kHz cannot recreate it. Google likewise recommends retaining the native rate and warns that low-rate A-law/μ-law audio can reduce recognition accuracy. [Google Speech-to-Text best practices](https://docs.cloud.google.com/speech-to-text/docs/best-practices).

2. **Model mismatch.** Nigerian pronunciation, names, numbers and children’s speech are underrepresented in general models. Independent African ASR studies report material degradation on African-accented dialogue, especially noisy conversation, while accent adaptation can significantly reduce errors. [AfriSpeech-MultiBench](https://aclanthology.org/2025.ijcnlp-long.190/), [AfriSpeech-Dialog](https://aclanthology.org/2025.naacl-long.426/), [accent-adaptation study](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-srw.1/).

3. **Short-answer endpointing.** “Thirty,” “fifteen,” and similar answers contain very little context. If voice-activity detection removes even the first or last 100–200 ms, the remaining vowel can resemble many other words.

4. **Handset, room and speaking level.** These vary considerably and matter more after the telephone network has already removed acoustic detail.

5. **Carrier impairment.** Packet loss, jitter, bad international routing and extra transcoding can damage a call, but we have not yet measured evidence that they are the dominant problem here. Twilio’s own Voice Insights documentation says handset, last-mile, carrier and intermediate-network failures all affect media; it can identify and sometimes reroute network problems, but it does not reconstruct lost speech or reliably diagnose all in-stream noise. [Twilio Voice Insights FAQ](https://www.twilio.com/docs/voice/voice-insights/frequently-asked-questions), [call summary documentation](https://www.twilio.com/docs/voice/voice-insights/call-summary).

## Who uses Africa’s Talking?

Africa’s Talking is primarily a regional communications infrastructure provider for developers and African businesses. Its published use cases include IVR, call centers, surveys, notifications, OTP and call blasting—not only NGOs. It currently offers voice in a limited set of African countries, including Nigeria. [Africa’s Talking Nigeria](https://nigeria.africastalking.com/), [voice-country coverage](https://help.africastalking.com/en/articles/11532624-countries-offering-voice-product), [call-center guidance](https://help.africastalking.com/en/articles/2900658-what-you-need-to-build-a-call-center).

It does not publish an audited breakdown showing exactly what percentage of its traffic comes from fintechs, NGOs or other sectors.

Large international NGOs commonly use a managed platform such as Viamo, or negotiate directly with mobile-network operators for toll-free short codes. Africa’s Talking can be an infrastructure component, but it is not usually the NGO program itself.

Viamo is the strongest comparison:

- Its platforms use national toll-free voice access on basic phones.
- It reports 20 active country platforms.
- It reported approximately 1.35 million monthly active users in Nigeria.
- Its Ask Viamo Anything system demonstrated open-ended spoken questions and generative answers in Zambia—5,000 users and about 46,000 questions.
- GSMA’s assessment nevertheless identifies local-language speech recognition accuracy as a core constraint; poor recognition causes callers to hang up or not return.

[Viamo 2025 impact](https://viamo.io/our-2025-impact/), [Ask Viamo Anything](https://viamo.io/ask-viamo-anything-ai/), [Nigeria user figures](https://viamo.io/viamo-platform/ceo-annual-letter-2023/), [GSMA voice-first GenAI study](https://www.gsma.com/solutions-and-impact/connectivity-for-good/mobile-for-development/gsma_resources/voice-first-generative-ai-for-impact-insights-from-viamos-ask-viamo-anything-pilot-in-zambia/).

The important distinction is that Viamo’s established Nigerian success is predominantly toll-free IVR and structured content. Its newer open-ended STT/GenAI evidence comes from Zambia. That is encouraging, but it does not prove that unrestricted conversational recognition is already solved in Nigeria.

## Has AI-analyzed customer calling worked in Nigeria?

Yes, in adjacent forms.

- **Access Bank and Avaya:** Access Bank says roughly 80% of customer interactions in its markets are voice. Its contact-center modernization combined conversational routing, speech analytics, voice biometrics and CRM integration. Reported results included voice response time falling to about 30 seconds, first-contact resolution rising from 70% to 74%, and CSAT rising from 54% to 64%. Those improvements came from routing and operations—not a claim that Avaya made PSTN audio acoustically clearer. [Access Bank case study](https://www.avaya.com/en/success-stories/access-bank/).

- **ARM Investment Managers and Intron:** Intron’s Africa-focused Sahara models are being used for Nigerian call-center analysis and agent feedback. Sahara specifically targets 8 kHz call-center recordings, African names and numbers, background noise and overlapping speakers. This is directly relevant to Sabi, although its published performance comparisons and ARM outcomes are presently vendor claims rather than independent business measurements. [Sahara v2](https://www.intron.io/sahara-v2/), [Intron](https://www.intron.io/).

- **Viamo Nigeria:** National voice services have operated at substantial scale, but primarily with structured IVR. Its open-ended voice AI work demonstrates feasibility while also confirming that ASR remains a bottleneck.
