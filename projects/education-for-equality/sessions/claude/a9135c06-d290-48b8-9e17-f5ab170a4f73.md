The p-values you see (0.001, 0.003) ARE the significance evidence.

The interesting thing: the *individual* coefficients aren't significant on their own:
- `oosc_p` alone: -0.048 (SE 0.037), t ≈ -1.30 → not sig
- `oosc_p_x_ng` alone: -0.065 (SE 0.062), t ≈ -1.05 → not sig

Economically this means: the SSA-wide OOSC effect is noisy, the Nigeria-deviation effect is noisy, but the *total Nigeria effect* is pinned down sharply.

## Claude · 2026-05-13T23:11:01.802Z

Here's what each column does — they're four progressively stricter specifications of the same model:

**Column (1) Pooled** — plain OLS, no country or time controls. Treats all SSA country-years as one big sample. Cleanest interpretation but **most biased**: any time-invariant country characteristic (institutions, geography, colonial history) gets absorbed into the OOSC coefficient.

**Column (2) Country FE** — adds country fixed effects. Now you're identifying off **within-country variation over time**. Each country has its own intercept, so cross-country differences in unobservables (corruption, climate, ethnic fractionalization) are washed out. The coefficient now answers: "when Nigeria's OOSC goes up, does Nigeria's growth go down?" — not "do high-OOSC countries grow slower than low-OOSC countries?"

**Column (3) Two-way FE** — adds **time fixed effects** on top of country FE. Now also removes anything that affected all SSA countries in the same year (commodity price shocks, global recessions, IMF policy waves). This is the standard panel growth specification.

**Column (4) +Nigeria Int.** — keeps two-way FE and adds an interaction `oosc_x_ng_ner` (OOSC × Nigeria dummy). Lets the Nigeria effect deviate from the SSA average. Reading the row: SSA-wide OOSC effect is -0.040; Nigeria's deviation is +0.088 → Nigeria's net OOSC effect = -0.040 + 0.088 ≈ +0.048 (the *opposite sign*, which is why you ran this interaction).

---

**The variables:**

- **`oosc_proxy_ger`** — your OOSC measure built as `100 − GER` (Gross Enrollment Ratio). When GER is below 100, the gap is your proxy for out-of-school share.
- **`investment`** — investment/GDP ratio (standard MRW regressor, the `s_k` term).
- **`pop_growth`** — population growth rate (the `n + g + δ` term in MRW; technically n alone here, with `g + δ ≈ 0.05` assumed constant).
- **`oosc_x_ng_ner`** — Nigeria-interaction (OOSC × Nigeria dummy). Tests whether Nigeria's OOSC-growth relationship differs from the SSA average.

**Story the columns tell together:** the OOSC effect strengthens as you add controls (−0.016 → −0.025 → −0.038), which is consistent with the cross-country comparison being *understated* by unobserved heterogeneity. Once you add the Nigeria interaction (col 4), Nigeria appears to deviate from the SSA pattern in a positive direction — worth a sentence in your discussion explaining why (likely data quality on Nigerian GER, or the Boko Haram era distorting the within-Nigeria series).

## Claude · 2026-05-13T23:12:30.411Z

Because V3-2 and V4-2 are measuring different things on different samples, and the V3-2 proxy is **contaminated specifically for Nigeria**.

**Three reasons the sign flips:**

**1. The OOSC variable is different.**
- V3-2 uses `100 − GER` (enrollment-gap proxy)
- V4-2 uses the UIS direct OOSC rate (children-of-school-age-not-in-school / population-of-school-age)

GER is enrollment in primary divided by *primary-age population*. In Nigeria it routinely exceeds 100 because of massive grade repetition and over-age enrollment (kids start late, repeat, leave, return). When GER > 100, `100 − GER` goes *negative* — meaningless as an "out of school" measure but mechanically dragged into your regression. The UIS direct rate doesn't have that defect. So V3-2's Nigeria observations are *systematically mismeasured* in a way V4-2's aren't.

**2. Annual vs 5-year averages.**
- V3-2: 739 annual obs
- V4-2: 217 obs (5-year averages)

Growth regressions are conventionally run on 5-year averages because annual GDP-per-worker growth has massive cyclical noise (oil-price shocks, currency crises, election years). Nigeria's annual series is especially volatile because of oil. 5-year averaging filters that out. In V3-2, Nigeria's GER changes slowly while its growth bounces from −5% to +8% year-to-year — that destroys signal.

**3. The Boko Haram / oil-crash period.**
2009–2016 in Nigeria: enrollment collapses in the Northeast (GER drops → `100−GER` rises = "more OOSC"), but at the same time growth crashes from the 2014 oil shock and 2016 recession. So in annual data, when your OOSC proxy goes UP, growth goes DOWN — that *should* give the expected negative coefficient. But the Nigeria-interaction term is identified *off the difference* from SSA average, and SSA was also crashing in 2014–2016 (commodity supercycle ending). The within-Nigeria deviation from the SSA mean doesn't carry the same signal.

**What to do about it:**

- **Add a footnote in the paper** explaining why V4-2 is your preferred specification: cleaner OOSC measurement (UIS direct rate, not GER residual) + 5-year averaging consistent with the growth literature.

## Claude · 2026-05-13T23:32:24.814Z

Three options, with the case for each:

## Option A: Primary OOSC throughout (recommended as headline)

**Why:**
- **Nigeria's binding constraint is foundational literacy.** Hanushek and Woessmann (2008, 2012, 2015) emphasize cognitive skills over years of schooling, but Pritchett (2013) and Glewwe, Maïga, and Zheng (2014) show that for low-income countries the marginal return on the *first six years* of education dominates anything that happens later. Nigeria's 18 million OOSC are overwhelmingly at the primary level (UNESCO 2024 reports primary OOSC ≈ 24%, lower secondary ≈ 21%, upper secondary ≈ 30%, but the absolute numbers are far larger for primary because of population pyramid weighting).
- **Conceptual cleanliness.** "Total OOSC" as conventionally constructed in UIS data is the count of children 6–17 out of school divided by population 6–17. It pools three age bands (primary 6–11, lower secondary 12–14, upper secondary 15–17) into a single rate that mixes structurally different populations. Primary OOSC is one well-defined cohort.
- **MRW alignment.** Mankiw, Romer, and Weil (1992) actually used the *secondary* enrollment rate as their human-capital proxy — but only because in their 1985-era cross-country sample, primary enrollment was already near-universal in most middle-income countries. In a 2024 Sub-Saharan African sample where primary access is *still* the binding margin, primary OOSC is the variable that carries the variation MRW were trying to identify. Using primary OOSC is in the spirit of MRW, not a departure from it.
- **Endogeneity caveat for secondary.** Secondary OOSC is partially mechanical from primary failure: a child who never finished primary cannot be "in" secondary. Adding secondary OOSC to the regressor double-counts the same children, biasing the coefficient upward in absolute value.
- **Sample size.** Primary-only is N = 217 in your V4-2 Col 1. Adding secondary loses 36 observations (N = 181) without changing the qualitative conclusion (β = −0.113 vs −0.125, p = 0.001 vs 0.003).

## Option B: Total OOSC throughout

**Case for:**
- Thomas and Burnett (2014) — your closest comparable — sums by-level OOSC costs across primary, lower secondary, and upper secondary to produce their 7.83% headline. Using total OOSC makes your estimate directly comparable to theirs without methodological footnotes.
- Captures the full extensive margin of educational exclusion.

**Case against:**
- The pooling problem above.
- The "lower secondary" age band (12–14) in Nigeria is the most policy-fragile (drop-out spike, female marriage age, child-labor demand). Including it conflates two distinct policy failures (never-enrolled vs dropped-out).

## Option C: Primary as headline + Total as Col 5 robustness (best for an 8–10 page paper)

This is the standard move in published applied work. You get:
- A clean theoretical interpretation (Option A).
- Direct comparability with Thomas-Burnett (Option B), via one robustness column.
- A "Basic Education OOSC" version (Option C variant) aligned with Nigeria's 1999 Universal Basic Education Act, which covers primary + JSS (ages 6–14) — this is the *policy-relevant* aggregation, not the full age-6–17 total. Many Nigeria-focused studies (Adeyemi 2011; Eregha, Irughe, and Edafe 2018) explicitly use "basic education" to align with the UBE statute.
