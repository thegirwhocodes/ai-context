# Research Memo for Naomi: ARDL Wrong-Sign Diagnosis & Remedies for Nigeria OOSC-GDP Paper

This is a substantive empirical concern, but it is also a *very common* finding in the Nigeria growth-regression literature, and there are well-established ways to defend or fix it. Below I work through each of your six questions with concrete citations, then end with a prioritized action list.

---

## 1. Why long-run and short-run signs flip in ARDL — and what "wrong-sign" cointegrating coefficients mean

The first thing to internalize: in an ARDL/ECM, the **long-run coefficient is a ratio of estimated parameters** (essentially −β_levels / α_ECT), not a direct OLS slope. With a small sample and an adjustment coefficient that is itself unusual (your −1.72), this ratio can become unstable and even change sign. So a sign-flip between Δy and y dynamics is not automatically a theoretical violation; it is often a *symptom* of one of three things:

**(a) Slow-moving trending variables and near-spurious behavior.** When both GDP per capita and OOSC are highly persistent (close to I(1)) and trend together over a short window, the long-run estimate captures *contemporaneous co-trending* rather than the causal structural relationship. Granger and Newbold (1974) showed that regressions among trending series produce "significant" but meaningless coefficients; Phillips (1986) formalized this as t- and F-statistics that diverge as T→∞. Cointegration only fixes this if the residual is genuinely stationary — and with N=20 the unit-root tests on residuals have very low power. Your significant long-run OOSC coefficient (+1.78) on a 20-observation sample is exactly the regime where these problems bite.

**(b) Pesaran-Shin-Smith degenerate case.** PSS (2001), pp. 289–326, explicitly warn about "degenerate" cointegration where the F-test rejects but the long-run relationship is not actually identified. The recommended diagnostic is to (i) confirm the t-statistic on the lagged level of the dependent variable (your ECT) is significantly negative *and* between 0 and −1 in absolute terms, and (ii) check that the long-run coefficients have signs consistent with theory. Failing either is grounds to suspect that the bounds test is producing a spurious "yes."

**(c) Ratio interpretation.** The long-run coefficient θ = −β/α where α is the ECT speed of adjustment. If α is poorly estimated (and yours is overshooting at −1.72), then θ is mechanically distorted. Kripfganz & Schneider (2020, *Oxford Bulletin*) discuss this and provide updated response-surface critical values precisely because the small-sample distribution of θ is non-standard.

**Bottom line for the paper:** A wrong-sign long-run coefficient on a slow-moving variable like OOSC, in a 20-observation sample, with an ECT below −1, is collectively a red flag that the *long-run cointegrating vector is not well identified*. The short-run coefficient (D.oosc = −6.52, theory-consistent) is more credible because it is a standard OLS coefficient on first-differenced (stationary) data — it does not depend on the cointegrating ratio.

## 2. Structural breaks in Nigeria 2000–2024

There are **at least three documented breaks** that the literature flags, and any of them could be biasing your levels regression:

**2008–2009: Global financial crisis + onset of Boko Haram insurgency.** Nwosa (2019) and several others using Zivot-Andrews and Lee-Strazicich endogenous-break unit root tests find a structural break in Nigerian macro variables in 2008. ACLED records 2,157 conflict events and 24,071 fatalities in the North-East between 2009 and 2016 (Bertoni, Di Maio, Molini, Nisticò, *Education is forbidden: The effect of the Boko Haram conflict on education in North-East Nigeria*, *Journal of Development Economics*, 2019 — World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 8978). Their difference-in-differences shows the conflict reduced school participation by 0.74 years in affected LGAs and disproportionately destroyed schools in Borno, Yobe, Adamawa, Bauchi, Gombe, Taraba. **This directly drives the OOSC numerator** — meaning Nigeria's national OOSC rate is partly a war indicator after 2009.

**2014q2–2014q3: Oil price collapse.** Akinleye & Ekpo (2013), Aminu & Aminu (2020), and the IMF 2015 Article IV all confirm Brent's collapse from ~$110 to ~$40 produced a structural break in Nigerian GDP, fiscal revenue, and the naira in mid-2014. Growth fell from 6.3% (2014) to 2.7% (2015) and Nigeria entered recession in 2016. *This is the single most important break for your paper* because it severs the historical co-movement between oil-rents and GDP that shows up in your long-run vector.

**2023: Tinubu fuel subsidy removal + FX unification.** Subsidies fell from 2.2% of GDP (2022) to 0.9% (2023). The naira moved from ~₦460/USD to ~₦1,700/USD between June 2023 and 2024. Headline inflation jumped from ~18% to over 24% (S&P Global; Afrobarometer AD958, 2023). If your sample ends in 2021 this is outside your window, but it is worth noting in robustness discussion as a reason not to extend to 2024 without a dummy.

**Recommendation:** A recent paper directly relevant — Onaolapo & co-authors (2024), *Structural Change and Growth Dynamics in Nigeria: Evidence from Break-Adjusted Cointegration Analysis* (ResearchGate working paper) — explicitly applies Gregory-Hansen and Hatemi-J cointegration tests with structural breaks for Nigeria and finds standard cointegration tests reject when breaks are accommodated. **Cite this paper as direct precedent for your strategy.**

## 4. Education-growth in resource-dependent economies

This is where your wrong-sign result is *least surprising*. The literature has a name for it: the **"human-capital channel of the resource curse."**

- **Gylfason (2001), "Natural resources, education, and economic development," *European Economic Review* 45(4–6): 847–859.** Original statement: oil-rich economies systematically under-invest in education because returns to skilled labor are depressed by Dutch disease and rents. Cross-country, he shows secondary-enrollment-to-resource-rents correlations are negative.
- **Cockx & Francken (2016), "Natural resources: A curse on education spending?" *Energy Policy* 92: 394–408.** Direct test of Gylfason. They find resource rents *do not* increase education spending, particularly in SSA. Highly relevant: Nigeria is a case in their sample.
- **Bhattacharyya & Hodler (2010, 2014)** — institutions condition the curse; with weak institutions (Nigeria's score is below average), education-growth links break down.
- **Stijns (2006), "Natural resource abundance and human capital accumulation," *World Development* 34(6): 1060–1083.** Finds that natural resource exports reduce educational attainment growth.
- **Birdsall, Pinckney & Sabot (2001)** in the Auty (ed.) *Resource Abundance and Economic Development* (Oxford UP) — explicit channel argument: Dutch disease lowers traded-sector demand for human capital.

**How to frame this in your paper:** "The positive long-run OOSC coefficient is consistent with a resource-curse human-capital channel (Gylfason 2001; Cockx & Francken 2016) wherein the marginal product of schooling is suppressed in oil-dependent economies, so reductions in OOSC do not translate into measured GDP gains over the sample window. The short-run coefficient (−6.52, p=0.014) recovers the standard human-capital prediction once we condition on differences." This *turns your weakness into a finding*.

## 5. The −1.72 ECT: overshooting or misspecification?

In the canonical reference (Banerjee, Dolado, Galbraith, Hendry 1993, *Co-integration, Error-Correction, and the Econometric Analysis of Non-Stationary Data*, Oxford UP), the ECT should lie in (−1, 0). Values **outside (−2, 0)** are unstable; values in **(−2, −1)** indicate *over-correction* — the system corrects more than 100% of last period's disequilibrium and then dampens back, producing oscillatory convergence.

**Three interpretations:**

1. **Genuine overshooting** — common in financial/exchange-rate models (Dornbusch overshooting), occasionally defensible for highly volatile macros. Possible for Nigerian GDP given oil-driven shocks, but you'd need to argue this explicitly.
2. **Mis-specified lag length** — the most common cause. Loose consensus on Statalist and Dave Giles' econometrics blog (Giles, 2013, "ARDL Models — Part II"): if ECT < −1, refit with a different lag selection criterion (try AIC vs. BIC vs. HQIC; try max lag = 1 vs. 2).
3. **No genuine cointegration** — the bounds test gave a false positive. Check the t-test on the lagged level (Pesaran-Shin-Smith case III/IV t-bound). If the t-stat is also outside the bounds and the F-stat is borderline, treat the cointegration claim with skepticism.

**Practical check for the paper:** Re-run with max lag = 1 (smallest possible — appropriate for N=20). If the ECT moves into (−1, 0), keep that specification. If it stays below −1, you must either justify overshooting theoretically or move to a different estimator.

## 6. Recommended remedies — prioritized for an undergrad paper with N=20

In order of bang-for-buck for your situation:

**(1) Drop variables for parsimony (highest priority).** With N=20 and 7 IVs, you have ~12 residual degrees of freedom *before* counting lags. The standard rule (Frees, *Longitudinal and Panel Data*; also widely used in growth-regression practice) is at least 10 obs/regressor; you are well below. Drop **inflation** and **life expectancy** (both highly correlated with trend/oil) and keep OOSC, investment, oil rents, trade, and one conflict/break control. This alone often resolves wrong-sign issues.

**(2) Add a 2014 step dummy (or 2016 — recession year).** This is the single most defensible structural change. Pesaran-Shin-Smith bounds with break dummies are well-established (see Sam, McNown & Goh, 2019, "An augmented autoregressive distributed lag bounds test for cointegration," *Economic Modelling* 80: 130–141). Nigeria-specific precedent: Salisu & Isah (2017) and the Onaolapo (2024) paper.

**(3) Use Narayan (2005) small-sample critical values, not PSS asymptotic.** Narayan, P.K. (2005), "The saving and investment nexus for China: Evidence from cointegration tests," *Applied Economics* 37(17): 1979–1990, provides bounds for T=30 to T=80 — the asymptotic PSS bounds *over-reject* in small samples. Your 20-obs sample is at the lower edge; use Narayan's T=30 bounds as the most conservative reasonable cutoff, or better yet use Kripfganz & Schneider (2020) response-surface approximations.

**(4) Robustness check with FMOLS and DOLS.** FMOLS (Phillips & Hansen 1990, *Review of Economic Studies* 57: 99–125) and DOLS (Stock & Watson 1993, *Econometrica* 61: 783–820) are alternative single-equation cointegration estimators. Stock-Watson DOLS is generally preferred in small samples per their original Monte Carlo results. *Reporting both FMOLS and DOLS alongside ARDL* is standard practice in current applied papers (e.g., the Springer 2023 SSA financial development paper) and is exactly what an external reviewer would ask for.

**(5) NARDL for oil rents only.** Shin, Yu & Greenwood-Nimmo (2014), in Horrace & Sickles (eds.), *Festschrift in Honor of Peter Schmidt*, Springer — separate oil rents into positive and negative partial sums. Oil price *increases* (boom) and *decreases* (bust) plausibly have asymmetric growth effects in an oil exporter; lumping them together can produce a wrong-sign linear coefficient. This is more advanced and probably overkill for a term paper, but cite it as "left for future work" — it shows methodological awareness.

**(6) Discuss endogeneity of OOSC explicitly.** Lee & Lee (2016, *Journal of Development Economics* 122: 147–169) provide the IV strategy (parental education lags) used in cross-country work. You almost certainly cannot implement IV in a 20-obs single-country setting, but acknowledging this caveat is important — and it gives you another reason to favor the short-run (first-differenced) coefficient interpretation, since differencing partially mitigates time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity.

## What to tell Naomi about defending her paper

The wrong-sign long-run OOSC coefficient is **defensible if she frames it carefully**:

- *Short-run coefficient (−6.52) is the one to emphasize* as her primary finding. It is theory-consistent, statistically significant, and on stationary differenced data it does not suffer the spurious-regression worry.
